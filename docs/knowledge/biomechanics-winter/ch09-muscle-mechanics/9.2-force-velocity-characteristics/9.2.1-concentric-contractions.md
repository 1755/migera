# 9.2.1 Concentric contractions

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §9.2.1, pp. 236–238 ·
> [open PDF at p. 236](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=249) ·
> Up: [9.2 Force-velocity characteristics](./INDEX.md)

A shortening muscle produces **less force the faster it shortens**, following
Hill's hyperbola: maximum (isometric) force P0 at zero velocity, falling to
zero at the maximum shortening velocity V0. The loss behaves like viscous
friction, which is why muscles act as built-in dampers.

## Key ideas

- **Isometric is a special case**: zero velocity on the force-velocity curve,
  which is itself measured at one fixed muscle length (length must be
  reported; length and velocity together form a surface, §9.2.3).
- **Activation family** (Fig. 9.12): curves at 25, 50, 75, 100% activation;
  100% is rare outside athletic bursts.
- **Two causes of the drop:** cross-bridges breaking and re-forming at shorter
  length, and fluid **viscosity** in contractile element and connective tissue.
  Net effect ≈ a fluid damper.
- **Diagnostic for which cause dominates:** if all viscosity were passive, the
  curve's slope would be independent of activation; if all were cross-bridge
  related, the slope would scale with activation. Green (1969) separated the
  two contributions from curve families.
- Hill's form (1938) was motivated by thermodynamics (shortening heat); it
  fits empirical data well **only for isotonic contractions near resting
  length**.

## Equations and numbers

**Fenn & Marsh (1935)**, Eq. 9.2, as printed in Winter:

$$V = V_0\,e^{P/B} - KP$$

$V$ shortening velocity at force $P$; $V_0$ unloaded shortening velocity;
$B, K$ constants. (Caution: the exponent is printed positive, which with
$B > 0$ gives $V > V_0$ for small $P$; Fenn & Marsh's original is usually
quoted as $e^{-P/B}$. Check the primary source before using it.)

**Hill (1938)**, Eq. 9.3:

$$(P + a)(V + b) = (P_0 + a)\,b$$

- $P_0$ maximum isometric tension; $a$ coefficient of shortening heat;
  $b = a\,V_0 / P_0$; $V_0$ maximum velocity (at $P = 0$).

Rearranged forms (algebra from Eq. 9.3, not printed in the book), with
$k = a/P_0 = b/V_0$:

$$P = \frac{a\,(V_0 - V)}{V + b}, \qquad
\frac{P}{P_0} = \frac{1 - V/V_0}{1 + V/(k\,V_0)}, \qquad
V = \frac{b\,(P_0 - P)}{P + a}$$

Winter gives no numeric value for $a/P_0$ (the curvature). Values around
0.25 are common in the wider literature — flagged here as **not from this
book**.

**Maximum shortening velocity:** about **6 l0/s** in animal experiments
(Faulkner et al., 1980; Close, 1965), where $l_0$ is resting fiber length;
likely low for humans — Woittiez (1984) estimated **> 10 l0/s** for soleus
from plantarflexion velocities above 8 rad/s and a 5 cm moment arm.

## Relevance to migera

The most useful equation in the chapter for the active ragdoll.
`pd_torque_at` in `src/character/anim/math/pd.rs` clamps the torque to a
**velocity-independent** `max_torque`. A Hill-shaped ceiling would make the
available torque shrink as the joint already rotates in the torque's
direction:

$$\tau_{\max}(\omega) = \tau_{\max,0}\,\frac{1 - \omega_\parallel/\omega_{\max}}{1 + \omega_\parallel/(k\,\omega_{\max})}, \quad \omega_\parallel = \omega \cdot \hat{\tau} > 0$$

with $\omega_\parallel$ the joint angular velocity component along the
commanded torque. Effects: fast swings saturate early (a natural speed
limit, no explicit clamp), and the character has less authority to accelerate
limbs that are already moving — which a player sees as weight. This is
"natural damping" from the actuator, distinct from the PD's `kd` term, and
does not touch the `kd·dt < 2` stability bound because it only lowers the
ceiling. $\omega_{\max}$ can be estimated from V0 via the moment arm
(Woittiez's 8 rad/s ankle). Not modeled today.
