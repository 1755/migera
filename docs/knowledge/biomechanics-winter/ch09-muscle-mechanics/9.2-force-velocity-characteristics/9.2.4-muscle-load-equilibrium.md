# 9.2.4 Combining muscle characteristics with load characteristics: equilibrium

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §9.2.4, pp. 240–243 ·
> [open PDF at p. 240](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=253) ·
> Up: [9.2 Force-velocity characteristics](./INDEX.md)

A muscle is always in equilibrium with its load: its **operating point is the
intersection of the muscle's characteristic and the load's characteristic**,
and in movement that point travels continuously over the force-length-velocity
surface. Real walking data show the path is a complex loop, not a trip along
any single textbook curve.

## Key ideas

- **Muscles are the body's only motors.** Loads may be static (holding a
  weight, pushing a wall or floor, isometric co-contraction where one muscle is
  the other's load) or dynamic (inertial, viscous) — usually a mix.
- Chapter 5's equations of motion already imposed equilibrium, but lumped all
  muscles at a joint into one **torque motor**; this section looks inside it.
- **Spring loads** (Fig. 9.14a): muscle starts at $l_1 > l_0$ with the spring
  at rest; at 50% activation it shortens to $l_2$ against a linear spring
  (point a) or to $l_3$ against a nonlinear spring (point b). Stiffer load →
  less shortening.
- **Gravity load** (Fig. 9.14b): elbow flexors lifting the forearm from
  vertical. Increasing activation moves the equilibrium through points a → e,
  with e at 100% activation. In transit the point also moves over the
  force-velocity characteristic, tracing a **3-D locus**.
- **Dynamic equilibrium in walking** (Fig. 9.15): ankle muscle moment vs.
  ankle angular velocity from heel contact to toe-off (small angle changes →
  length ∝ angle, velocity ∝ angular velocity, tendon force ∝ moment):
  1. Heel contact → foot flat: **dorsiflexors** active while the foot
     plantarflexes — negative work lowering the foot.
  2. After foot flat: **plantarflexors** dominate, slowing the leg rotating
     over the fixed foot — negative work again.
  3. A few frames after heel-off: plantarflexor moment with plantarflexing
     velocity — **positive work, active push-off**, where most of the new energy
     enters the body, ending at toe-off.

## Equations and numbers

Read off Fig. 9.15 (approximate, figure only): net ankle moment up to roughly
**130–140 N·m** (plantarflexor) near zero angular velocity around heel-off;
angular velocity spans about **−2 rad/s (dorsiflexion) to +4 rad/s
(plantarflexion)**; the dorsiflexor moment stays below ~40 N·m. The figure
also draws iso-activation lines labelled 20% and 40% contraction of the
plantarflexors.

**Stability** is not discussed in the book. A standard reading of Fig. 9.14
(added here): an equilibrium is stable when, around the intersection, the
muscle's force falls with length relative to the load's — so a small
disturbance produces a net restoring force. On the descending limb of the
active curve (beyond l0) the muscle alone is negatively stiff and relies on
the load or the parallel element for stability.

## Relevance to migera

- **PD as an equilibrium-point controller:** migera's per-joint PD
  (`src/character/anim/math/pd.rs`) is itself a "muscle characteristic" —
  torque as a function of angle error and velocity — and where a ragdoll joint
  actually settles under gravity is the intersection with the load, not the
  target. That is why a saturated or weakened joint sags to a new equilibrium
  instead of reaching its target; the equilibrium view predicts the sag
  angle from ceiling vs. gravity moment.
- **Gait test oracle:** Fig. 9.15 says ankle stance is negative work, then
  positive work at push-off. If migera ever measures joint power
  ($\tau\cdot\omega$) on the ragdoll ankle while walking
  (`src/character/anim/gait.rs`, `ragdoll_plugin.rs`), the sign sequence
  −, −, + across stance is a qualitative check that push-off is driven.
- **Scale check:** ~140 N·m peak ankle moment is the real-human magnitude a
  ragdoll ankle ceiling would need (times the body-mass ratio) to support
  push-off. migera's ceilings are angular accelerations, not N·m (see the
  avian note below), so convert through inertia before comparing.
