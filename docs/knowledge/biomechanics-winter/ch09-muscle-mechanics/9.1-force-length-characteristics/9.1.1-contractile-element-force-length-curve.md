# 9.1.1 Force-length curve of the contractile element

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §9.1.1, pp. 231–232 ·
> [open PDF at p. 231](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=244) ·
> Up: [9.1 Force-length characteristics of muscles](./INDEX.md)

The active (contractile) element produces its **maximum isometric tension at
resting length l0**, where actin–myosin overlap gives the most cross-bridges,
and less on either side: stretched, the filaments pull apart until no
cross-bridges remain; shortened, the filaments overlap and interfere.

## Key ideas

- Mechanism (Gordon et al., 1966; Fig. 9.7 draws the filament overlap above
  each point of the curve):
  - **Resting length, sarcomere ≈ 2.5 µm:** maximum cross-bridges, maximum
    tension.
  - **Lengthening:** fewer cross-bridges, tension falls; at **≈ 4.0 µm** there
    are none and active tension is **zero**.
  - **Shortening below rest:** cross-bridges overlap and interfere; tension
    falls until full overlap at **≈ 1.5 µm**, where it is drastically reduced
    but **not zero**.
- The curve is roughly an inverted U, drawn as "% max. isometric tension"
  against contractile-element length; it is asymmetric about l0 (the short
  side does not reach zero).
- The book gives no equation for the curve — only its shape and the three
  sarcomere anchors.

## Equations and numbers

Relative to resting sarcomere length 2.5 µm, the anchors are **0.6 l0** (1.5
µm, strongly reduced), **1.0 l0** (peak), **1.6 l0** (4.0 µm, zero). These
ratios are computed from the book's sarcomere lengths; a common modeling
choice (not from Winter) is a Gaussian or parabola in $l/l_0$ fitted to them.

## Where to read in the book

- p. 231 (PDF 244): section start.
- p. 232 (PDF 245): Fig. 9.7 curve with filament-overlap sketches; the
  1.5/2.5/4.0 µm explanation.

## Relevance to migera

migera's PD ceilings (`default_joint_params` in
`src/character/anim/ragdoll.rs`) are **independent of joint angle**: a hip is
as strong fully flexed as at mid-range. Real joint strength varies with angle
because each muscle rides its force-length curve (and its moment arm changes,
see [9.1.4](./9.1.4-in-vivo-force-length.md)). An angle-dependent ceiling
multiplier — peaked near the joint's mid-range, falling toward its limits —
would make a ragdoll weaker at extreme poses (a deep squat is hard to hold,
a nearly straight arm pushes well). Not modeled today; it would need
per-joint data this book does not give (see 9.1.4 for the one in vivo curve
it quotes).
