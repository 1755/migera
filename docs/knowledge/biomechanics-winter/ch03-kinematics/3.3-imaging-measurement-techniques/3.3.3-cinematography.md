# 3.3.3 Cinematography

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §3.3.3, pp. 55–58 ·
> [open PDF at p. 55](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=68) ·
> Up: [3.3 Imaging measurement techniques](./INDEX.md)

16-mm high-speed film was the standard compromise between image size and
cost. Exposure is set by film speed, shutter factor, frame rate and f-stop;
the frame rate must satisfy the sampling theorem. Coordinates were digitized
by hand from projected frames, slowly but with small, random error — the
1–1.5 mm rms noise that the book's smoothing chapter is built around.

## Key ideas

- **Formats:** 8 mm too small for accuracy; 35/70 mm too expensive; 16 mm is
  the norm for high-speed cameras (spring-, battery- or mains-driven).
- **Film speed:** ASA rating; 4-X reversal at ASA 400 is common. Faster films
  are grainier and hurt quantitative accuracy.
- **Shutter:** a rotating shutter exposes each frame during its open arc
  (Fig. 3.10). Exposure time = 1/(frame rate × shutter factor); e.g. 60 fps
  with a 3-factor shutter gives 1/180 s.
- **Setup order:** preset film ASA, shutter factor and frame rate (high enough
  for the event per the sampling theorem, low enough to save light and film);
  then pick the f-stop from a light meter.
- **Digitizing (§3.3.3.1):** each frame is projected; an operator aligns cross
  hairs (or touches a grid with a pen) on each landmark and sends x, y to a
  computer (Fig. 3.11). Throughput ≈ 15 coordinate pairs/min (the Fig. 3.11
  caption says about 10), so a 3 s record at 50 fps with five markers takes
  about 30 min.
- **Digitizing error** is random and small: **1–1.5 mm rms** with the camera
  4 m from the subject (Fig. 3.11 caption: about 1 mm rms).

§3.4's differentiation problem concrete, and the pointer to the sampling
theorem applies to any fixed-rate sampling of a motion signal, including a
fixed-timestep simulation.
