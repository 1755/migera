# 3.1.2 Total description of a body segment in space

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §3.1.2, pp. 47–48 ·
> [open PDF at p. 47](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=60) ·
> Up: [3.1 Kinematic conventions](./INDEX.md)

A rigid segment is fully described by **15 time-varying variables**; a
12-segment body needs 180. Symmetry, a single plane and lumping the head,
arms and trunk into one "HAT" segment cut sagittal gait down to 36.

## Key ideas

The 15 variables per segment:

1. Position $(x, y, z)$ of the segment centre of mass.
2. Linear velocity $(\dot x, \dot y, \dot z)$ of the COM.
3. Linear acceleration $(\ddot x, \ddot y, \ddot z)$ of the COM.
4. Segment angle in two planes, $\theta_{xy}, \theta_{yz}$.
5. Angular velocity in two planes, $\omega_{xy}, \omega_{yz}$.
6. Angular acceleration in two planes, $\alpha_{xy}, \alpha_{yz}$.

- **Two angles suffice** for a segment's *direction*: the third planar angle
  is redundant. (This describes the long axis only; axial twist about the
  segment is not captured. Chapter 7 adds full 3D orientation.)
- **Whole body:** feet, legs, thighs, trunk, head, upper arms, forearms+hands
  = 12 segments → 15 × 12 = **180 variables**.
- **Standard simplification for level walking:** assume sagittal-plane
  motion, ignore arms, treat head-arms-trunk as one segment (**HAT**), and
  assume left/right symmetry so only one limb is recorded → 4 segments in one
  plane → **36 variables**.

## Where to read in the book

- pp. 47–48 (PDF 60–61): the 15-variable list, the 180 count, and the HAT/symmetry reduction.

## Relevance to migera

Useful as a checklist of what a "segment state" is. migera's rotation-space
stack stores local rotations and derives positions by forward kinematics, so
Winter's 15-variable segment state is what a debugging dump or a
BRP-based verification of one bone would reconstruct (position, velocity and
acceleration of the COM plus orientation and its derivatives). The two-angle
description's blindness to twist is a reminder that a direction-only check
cannot catch roll errors on a bone. The 4-segment HAT model is the same
reduction the book's Appendix A walking data uses.
