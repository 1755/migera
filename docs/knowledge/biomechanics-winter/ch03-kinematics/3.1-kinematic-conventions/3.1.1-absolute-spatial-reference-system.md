# 3.1.1 Absolute spatial reference system

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §3.1.1, pp. 46–47 ·
> [open PDF at p. 46](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=59) ·
> Up: [3.1 Kinematic conventions](./INDEX.md)

Anatomical words (proximal, flexion, anterior) are relative and say nothing
about gravity or where the body is in space, so Winter fixes one absolute
frame for the whole book: **X forward (progression), Y up, Z medial-lateral**,
with every angle given a zero direction and a positive sense
(**counterclockwise positive**), and every velocity/acceleration signed by
the same rule. Signs, not magnitudes, carry direction.

## Key ideas

- **Why an absolute frame.** Relative (anatomical) terms describe one limb
  against another. Analysing movement against the ground or gravity needs a
  fixed spatial frame. Imaging systems give absolute data naturally;
  body-mounted instruments (goniometers, accelerometers, §3.2) give relative
  data and lose gravity and the direction of travel.
- **Axes (Fig. 3.1).** Y vertical, X anterior-posterior (direction of
  progression), Z medial-lateral. This is the frame commonly used for gait.
  Planes: sagittal = XY, frontal = YZ, transverse = XZ.
- **Angle conventions.**
  - In the XY (sagittal) plane: 0° along +X, positive counterclockwise.
  - In the YZ plane: 0° along +Y, positive counterclockwise.
- **Derivative conventions follow automatically** if the coordinates keep
  their signs: $\dot x$ is positive when x increases, $\ddot x$ positive when
  $\dot x$ increases (same for y, z). A counterclockwise increase of angle is
  positive ω; ω increasing gives positive α.
- **Reading signs, worked example** (right leg segment of a walking subject,
  viewed from the right side):

  | ω | α | $v_x$ | $a_x$ | $v_y$ | $a_y$ |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | −2.34 rad/s | 14.29 rad/s² | 0.783 m/s | −9.27 m/s² | 0.021 m/s | −0.31 m/s² |

  Meaning: the leg rotates clockwise but is decelerating (α opposes ω, i.e.
  accelerating counterclockwise); its centre of mass moves forward and very
  slightly up, while decelerating forward and accelerating downward. The
  point: a negative acceleration is not "slowing down" unless its sign
  opposes the velocity's.
- **Viewing side matters (derived, not stated in the book).** With X forward
  and Y up in a right-handed frame, +Z points to the subject's *right*, so
  "counterclockwise" means counterclockwise as seen from the subject's right
  side, with the subject walking left-to-right in the image. That matches the
  book's right-side camera view throughout. Viewed from the left side, the
  same motion reads clockwise; a sign convention is incomplete until the
  viewing side (the axis the rotation is about) is fixed.

## Where to read in the book

- p. 46 (PDF 59): why conventions are needed; axes; angle and derivative sign rules.
- p. 47 (PDF 60): Fig. 3.1 (axes and body planes); the worked leg example.

## Relevance to migera

Directly relevant to a class of bugs migera has already paid for. A walk ran
backward and knees bent backward for weeks because a rotation's sign was
assumed without fixing the rig's facing, and 30+ tests measured an unsigned
angle that cannot see direction. Winter's discipline is the fix: every angle
has a stated zero, a stated positive sense and a stated viewing axis, and
velocities/accelerations inherit the sign. Note that migera's frames differ:
Bevy is Y-up and right-handed like Winter's, but `puppet_base.gltf` faces
+Z and the synthetic rig faces −Z, so Winter's "+X = forward" must be
translated through the rig's own forward vector, never hardcoded. The
worked example is a ready reading-comprehension check for signed kinematic
test assertions.
