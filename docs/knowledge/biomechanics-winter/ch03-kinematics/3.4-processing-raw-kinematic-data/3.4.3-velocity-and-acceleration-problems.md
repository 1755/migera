# 3.4.3 Problems of calculating velocities and accelerations

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §3.4.3, pp. 66–67 ·
> [open PDF at p. 66](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=79) ·
> Up: [3.4 Processing of raw kinematic data](./INDEX.md)

Differentiating a signal multiplies each harmonic's amplitude by its
frequency, and differentiating twice by the frequency squared. Noise that is
invisible in position therefore dominates acceleration: in gait data a 20th
harmonic at 1/100 of the fundamental ends up **4 times** the fundamental in
the acceleration. Smooth before you differentiate.

## Key ideas

- Represent the signal as a sum of harmonics (Eq. 3.3); differentiate term by
  term (Eqs. 3.4–3.5). Velocity amplitudes grow **linearly** with harmonic
  number, acceleration amplitudes with its **square** (Fig. 3.17: equal
  displacement amplitudes at harmonics 1, 2, 3 become 1:2:3 in velocity and
  1:4:9 in acceleration).
- Worked gait example: $x_1 = 5$ cm, $x_{20} = 0.5$ mm (1% of the
  fundamental, "hardly perceptible" in displacement). Velocity: ×20 → the
  20th harmonic is 1/5 of the fundamental. Acceleration: ×400 → it is 4× the
  fundamental. Fig. 3.19 shows the resulting raw vs filtered toe acceleration.

## Equations and numbers

$$x = \sum_{n=1}^{N} X_n \sin(n\omega_0 t + \theta_n) \quad (3.3)$$

$$V_x = \frac{dx}{dt} = \sum_{n=1}^{N} n\omega_0 X_n \cos(n\omega_0 t + \theta_n) \quad (3.4)$$

$$A_x = \frac{dV_x}{dt} = -\sum_{n=1}^{N} (n\omega_0)^2 X_n \sin(n\omega_0 t + \theta_n) \quad (3.5)$$

$\omega_0$ = fundamental frequency, $n$ = harmonic number, $X_n$, $\theta_n$ =
amplitude and phase of the nth harmonic.

**Derived (not in the book; own calculation, checked numerically).** For
white noise of standard deviation σ on each sample, the finite-difference
formulas of §3.5 amplify it by fixed factors:

| Formula | Noise std | σ = 1 mm, Δt = 1/60 s |
|---|---|---|
| central velocity $(x_{i+1}-x_{i-1})/2\Delta t$ (3.15) | $\sigma/(\sqrt2\,\Delta t)$ | 0.042 m/s |
| 3-point acceleration $(x_{i+1}-2x_i+x_{i-1})/\Delta t^2$ (3.18c) | $\sqrt6\,\sigma/\Delta t^2$ | 8.8 m/s² |
| 5-point acceleration (3.15 applied twice) $(x_{i+2}-2x_i+x_{i-2})/4\Delta t^2$ | $\sqrt6\,\sigma/(4\Delta t^2)$ | 2.2 m/s² |

Doubling the sample rate doubles the velocity noise and quadruples the
acceleration noise — the reason §3.4.4.4 says the optimal cutoff depends on
$f_s$. For comparison, the toe's filtered horizontal acceleration in walking
peaks near ±20 m/s² (Fig. 3.19, TV data), while the raw-data curve swings by
roughly ±5–10 m/s² between those peaks: millimetre noise is already a large
fraction of the signal.

## Relevance to migera

Directly applies wherever migera derives a velocity or acceleration from
sampled positions or rotations: `footlock::annotate_contacts` computes toe
speed by central differences, ragdoll/PD debugging compares body angular
velocities, and tests assert "zero world velocity while locked" or smooth
motion across a seam. Any sub-millimetre jitter (f32 rounding, IK clamps,
contact switching) is multiplied by $1/\Delta t$ in velocity and
$1/\Delta t^2$ in acceleration, so at 60 Hz tolerances on derived quantities
must be set from this gain, not from the position tolerance. For a test, it
also explains why a finite-difference acceleration check is fragile unless
the step is chosen deliberately (see the seam-continuity lesson).
