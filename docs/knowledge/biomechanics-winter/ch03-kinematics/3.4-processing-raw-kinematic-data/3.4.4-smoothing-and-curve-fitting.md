# 3.4.4 Smoothing and curve fitting of data

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §3.4.4, pp. 67–73 ·
> [open PDF at p. 67](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=80) ·
> Up: [3.4 Processing of raw kinematic data](./INDEX.md)

Contents: [Curve fitting](#curve-fitting-3441) · [Butterworth coefficients](#the-filter-3442) ·
[Dual pass](#dual-pass-zero-lag-filtering) · [Residual analysis](#choosing-the-cutoff-residual-analysis-3443) ·
[Optimal cutoff](#optimal-cutoff-for-derivatives-34444) · [Relevance](#relevance-to-migera)

Four ways to remove noise: polynomial, harmonic and spline curve fits, and —
the book's standard — **low-pass digital filtering**. A 2nd-order Butterworth
filter run once forward and once backward in time cancels its phase lag and
gives a 4th-order **zero-lag** filter; its cutoff is corrected (C = 0.802) so
the combined response lands where intended. The cutoff itself is chosen by
**residual analysis**: where the filtered-minus-raw residual stops behaving
like pure noise. For walking, about 6 Hz.

## Curve fitting (3.4.4.1)

- **Polynomial** $x(t) = a_0 + a_1t + a_2t^2 + \dots + a_nt^n$ (3.6), fit by
  least squares. Suits some non-repetitive movements (broad jump), but see
  §3.4.5: a 9th-order fit gave badly wrong accelerations.
- **Harmonic** $x(t) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n\sin(n\omega_0t+\theta_n)$
  (3.7), keeping the lowest N harmonics. Better founded for repetitive
  movement, but assumes stationary $a_n, \theta_n$ (a severe assumption,
  §2.2.4.5).
- **Spline**: piecewise polynomials joined at inflection points. The
  inflection points must be found from the noisy data, so the noise being
  removed corrupts the fit.

## The filter (3.4.4.2)

Digital filtering is not a curve fit: it attenuates noise because signal and
noise occupy different frequencies. Recursive difference equation (Eq. 2.18,
Chapter 2), with $X$ raw and $X'$ filtered:

$$X'_n = a_0X_n + a_1X_{n-1} + a_2X_{n-2} + b_1X'_{n-1} + b_2X'_{n-2}$$

Coefficients for a 2nd-order filter (sampling rate $f_s$, cutoff $f_c$):

$$\omega_c = \frac{\tan(\pi f_c/f_s)}{C} \quad (3.8)$$

$$K_1 = \sqrt2\,\omega_c \ \text{(Butterworth)} \quad\text{or}\quad K_1 = 2\omega_c \ \text{(critically damped)}$$

$$K_2 = \omega_c^2,\quad a_0 = \frac{K_2}{1+K_1+K_2},\quad a_1 = 2a_0,\quad a_2 = a_0$$

$$K_3 = \frac{2a_0}{K_2},\quad b_1 = -2a_0 + K_3,\quad b_2 = 1 - 2a_0 - K_3 \ \ (= 1 - a_0 - a_1 - a_2 - b_1)$$

The book prints the first line as "$K = \sqrt2\omega_c$" but uses $K_1$ in
$a_0$; they are the same quantity. $C$ is the pass-count correction ($C = 1$
for a single pass). The $b$ terms are **added** (sign convention of Eq.
2.18); $a_0+a_1+a_2+b_1+b_2 = 1$ gives unity gain at DC. These coefficients
equal `scipy.signal.butter(2, fc/(fs/2))` exactly (with $b_i = -a_i^{\text{scipy}}$);
e.g. $f_s$ = 60 Hz, $f_c$ = 6 Hz, C = 1: $a_0$ = 0.067455, $b_1$ = 1.142981,
$b_2$ = −0.412802.

## Dual pass (zero-lag filtering)

- A 2nd-order filter has **90° phase lag at $f_c$**, distorting the timing of
  high harmonics within the passband ("phase distortion") — possibly worse
  than the amplitude distortion in the transition band.
- Fix (Winter et al., 1974): filter the once-filtered data again **in reverse
  time**. The equal and opposite phase lead gives zero net phase, and the
  roll-off is twice as sharp: a **4th-order zero-phase** filter.
- Two passes lower the effective cutoff to about **80%** of the single-pass
  value (Fig. 3.18), so the single-pass cutoff is set higher by the
  correction factor:
  - Butterworth: $C = (2^{1/n} - 1)^{0.25}$, $n$ = number of passes;
    dual pass **C = 0.802**.
  - Critically damped: $C = (2^{1/2n} - 1)^{0.5}$; dual pass **C = 0.435**.

  (Checked numerically: with C = 0.802 the dual-pass amplitude at $f_c$ is
  0.707, i.e. −3 dB exactly at the requested cutoff; without it, 0.5.)
- **Butterworth vs critically damped:** Butterworth has a slight overshoot to
  step/impulse inputs but much shorter rise time; critically damped has no
  overshoot but slower rise. Impulsive inputs are rare in movement data, so
  Butterworth is preferred.
- Fig. 3.19: toe horizontal acceleration by finite differences from raw data
  (noise-dominated) vs from data filtered at $f_c$ = 6 Hz (repeatable, passes
  through the middle of the raw curve, no lag).

```text
fn butter2(fc, fs, passes):           # Winter Eq. 3.8 and following
  C  = (2^(1/passes) - 1)^0.25        # 1 for a single pass, 0.802 for two
  wc = tan(pi*fc/fs) / C
  K1 = sqrt(2)*wc;  K2 = wc^2
  a0 = K2/(1+K1+K2);  a1 = 2*a0;  a2 = a0
  K3 = 2*a0/K2;  b1 = -2*a0 + K3;  b2 = 1 - 2*a0 - K3
fn filtfilt(x): y = pass(x); y = reverse(pass(reverse(y)))
  where pass: y[n] = a0*x[n] + a1*x[n-1] + a2*x[n-2] + b1*y[n-1] + b2*y[n-2]
```

The book does not discuss start-up transients at the record ends; in
practice (not from the book) records are padded (e.g. reflected) before
filtering and the padding discarded.

## Choosing the cutoff: residual analysis (3.4.4.3)

- A harmonic analysis (Fig. 3.16) can pick a cutoff but assumes an ideal,
  infinitely sharp filter. Better (Wells and Winter, 1980): filter at many
  cutoffs and plot the rms residual between raw and filtered data, which
  includes the real filter's transition band.

$$R(f_c) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(X_i - \hat X_i\right)^2} \quad (3.9)$$

  $X_i$ raw, $\hat X_i$ filtered with the 4th-order zero-lag filter at $f_c$,
  N samples.
- **Reading the plot (Fig. 3.20):** pure random noise gives a straight line
  falling from intercept **a** at 0 Hz to zero at Nyquist ($0.5f_s$); **a** is
  the rms of the noise (a 0 Hz filter outputs the mean). Fit that line (**de**)
  to the high-frequency end of the real residual. At low cutoffs the residual
  rises above the line: that excess is signal distortion. Choosing equal
  distortion and passed noise: draw a horizontal from **a** to meet the
  residual curve at **b**; its frequency is $f_c$; **bc** is both the
  distortion and the noise passed.
- **Gait data (Fig. 3.21):** four markers (rib, hip, heel, ball), horizontal
  and vertical, digitized from film at 5 m (image 2 m × 3 m). Noise line fit
  over harmonics 11–20: **Residual (mm) = 1.8 − 0.05 n**, r = 0.79. So noise
  is **1.8 mm rms** (under 1 part in 1000 of the field), the same for all
  markers and both coordinates. Heel and ball carry signal up to ~6 Hz; rib
  and hip vertical only ~3 Hz, so each marker can get its own cutoff.

## Optimal cutoff for derivatives (3.4.4.4)

- Equal distortion and noise is optimal for **displacement** only. Giakas and
  Baltzopoulos (1997) found the optimum depends on noise level and on whether
  displacement, velocity or acceleration is wanted (Winter doubts their
  harmonic-reconstructed reference signal).
- Yu et al. (1999): the optimal cutoff for accelerations is somewhat
  **higher** than the displacement residual choice, and depends on $f_s$
  (higher $f_s$ → smaller Δt → more finite-difference noise):

$$f_{c,2} = 0.06f_s - 0.000022f_s^2 + 5.95/\varepsilon \quad (3.10)$$

  $\varepsilon$ = relative mean residual between $X_i$ and $\hat X_i$. Winter
  notes their example curves lag, suggesting a single-pass 2nd-order filter
  was used instead of the zero-lag one.

## Relevance to migera

The tool to reach for whenever migera needs a *derived* motion quantity from
a sampled signal offline: toe speed for contact annotation
(`footlock::annotate_contacts` currently differences raw positions and then
median-filters the boolean result), root or COM velocity from a recorded
walk, joint angular velocities estimated from a pose sequence, or smoothing a
recorded clip before foot-slide cleanup. Zero lag matters: a causal
(single-pass) filter delays contact edges by a phase-dependent amount, which
is exactly the edge inaccuracy the `annotate_contacts` doc comment attributes
to the causal runtime detector. At runtime only causal filters are possible;
migera's critically damped springs are the causal 2nd-order low-pass, and
this note's critically-damped-vs-Butterworth trade-off (no overshoot vs
faster rise) is the same one a spring's damping ratio makes. Residual
analysis doubles as a test oracle: fed a clean synthetic walk plus known
white noise of σ mm, the residual line's intercept must come back ≈ σ.
