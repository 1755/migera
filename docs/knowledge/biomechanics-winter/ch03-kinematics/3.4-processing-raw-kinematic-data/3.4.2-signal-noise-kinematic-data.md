# 3.4.2 Signal versus noise in kinematic data

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §3.4.2, pp. 65–66 ·
> [open PDF at p. 65](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=78) ·
> Up: [3.4 Processing of raw kinematic data](./INDEX.md)

In walking, marker trajectories are periodic at the stride frequency, so the
signal lives in its low harmonics: over 99% of the power of even the fastest
markers (toe, heel) is below the 7th harmonic (~6 Hz). Content above that
looks like random noise, and noise is usually the high-frequency part.

## Key ideas

- **Frequency content depends on the marker and the task.** The vertical heel
  coordinate in running has higher frequencies than the knee or trunk;
  running has higher content than walking.
- **Repetitive movement → harmonics.** At 120 steps/min (2 Hz step rate) the
  stride frequency is 1 Hz, so expect components at 1, 2, 3, 4 … Hz.
- **Measured (Winter et al., 1974):** harmonic analysis of seven leg/foot
  markers in normal walking; toe and heel have the highest harmonics, and
  **99.7% of signal power is in the lowest seven harmonics (below 6 Hz)**.
- **Noise definition:** components of the final signal not due to the
  process itself. Above the 7th harmonic, the remaining power has noise
  characteristics, extending to the 20th harmonic (the highest analysed).
  If the error sources are random, the noise is additive and mostly high
  frequency.

## Equations and numbers

Fig. 3.16 (= Fig. 2.17): normalized harmonic amplitude of the toe marker's
vertical displacement, 20 subjects, log scale 0.001–1.0, fundamental = 1.00,
harmonics 0–20, mean and +2 s.d. The amplitude falls by roughly two decades
by the 7th harmonic and flattens into a noise floor beyond it.

## Where to read in the book

- p. 65 (PDF 78): stride harmonics and the 99.7% / 7-harmonic result, Fig. 3.16.
- p. 66 (PDF 79): definition of noise and its high-frequency character.

## Relevance to migera

Gives a real bandwidth target for procedural gait: a walk at ~1 Hz stride
needs only its first ~7 harmonics, so foot and pelvis trajectories should not
carry significant energy above ~6 Hz. Energy above that in an animated
trajectory (from contact snaps, IK clamps or phase seams) is, by the book's
definition, noise, and will dominate any velocity or acceleration derived
from it. A spectrum check of a walk cycle's toe trajectory against this
result is a cheap realism/regression test.
