# 3.2.3 Accelerometers

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §3.2.3, pp. 50–53 ·
> [open PDF at p. 50](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=63) ·
> Up: [3.2 Direct measurement techniques](./INDEX.md)

An accelerometer is a force transducer pressed by a known internal mass, so
its signal is proportional to acceleration — but only the component normal to
its face, measured in the limb's own rotating frame. The same world-space
acceleration gives different readings as the limb rotates, so accelerometers
suit only movements whose direction in space changes little.

## Key ideas

- **Principle:** $F = ma$ with $m$ known and constant; a strain-gauge or
  piezoresistive transducer converts F to a voltage V ∝ a. Acceleration away
  from the face reverses the sign.
- **Only the normal component** $a_n$ is measured (Fig. 3.5); the tangential
  component $a_t$ and total $a$ are unknown unless a **triaxial**
  accelerometer (three orthogonal units) is used.
- **Rotation problem** (Fig. 3.6): even a triaxial unit reports acceleration
  in the segment's frame; two limb postures with identical world
  acceleration give very different $a_n$. Usable for movements whose spatial
  direction changes little, or contrived ones (horizontal forearm flexion
  about a fixed elbow).
- **Bridge circuit** (Fig. 3.7): two fixed resistors $R_1$ and two
  transducer resistors $R_a$, $R_b$ form a half-bridge; at zero acceleration
  $R_a = R_b = R_1$ and, with the balance potentiometer set, V = 0.
  Acceleration raises one and lowers the other, giving V of sign matching
  direction. A mis-set balance potentiometer adds an offset (dashed line).
- **Advantages:** immediate signal.
- **Disadvantages:** acceleration relative to the mounting location; cost of
  many units and recorders; encumbrance; fragile under shock; the unit's own
  mass causes movement artefact in fast or impact movements.

## Relevance to migera

Low as hardware. Two transferable points. First, a frame-of-measurement trap:
a quantity read in a body's local frame (like avian body-local velocities or
a bone-local offset) changes with the body's rotation even when the world
quantity does not; convert to world space before comparing. Second, the book
later uses an accelerometer as an *independent* reference to validate
acceleration computed from positions (§3.4.5) — the same pattern as checking a
finite-difference result against an analytic or independently measured one.
