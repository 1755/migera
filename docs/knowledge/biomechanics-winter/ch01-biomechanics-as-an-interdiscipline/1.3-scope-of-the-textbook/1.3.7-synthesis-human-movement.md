# 1.3.7 Synthesis of human movement

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §1.3.7, p. 12 ·
> [open PDF at p. 12](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=25) ·
> Up: [1.3 Scope of the textbook](./INDEX.md)

Most biomechanics uses inverse solutions. **Synthesis** is the reverse. It
takes the same kind of model, drives it with assumed joint moments or
muscle forces, and predicts the kinematics. The payoff is asking "what
would happen if?" for cases that cannot be tried on a human.

## Key ideas

- Inverse solutions produce reaction forces, moments, energy and power,
  none of which can be measured in humans. Synthesis goes from those
  forcing functions back to motion.
- Potential uses: predict the effect of abnormal motor patterns, and
  search for optimal ones.
- Winter's 2009 verdict: usefulness so far has been **very poor** and
  limited to simple movements. The models lacked correct anthropometrics
  and enough degrees of freedom. It is still taught because of the
  potential payoff.

## Where to read in the book

- p. 12 (PDF 25): the whole section.

## Relevance to migera

High in concept. migera's active ragdoll *is* a synthesis model: PD
torques are the forcing functions and avian integrates the kinematics.
Winter's warning applies directly. A forward model is only as good as its
anthropometrics and degrees of freedom, so wrong masses or inertias, or
missing joints, make the output diverge from real motion. That is why
the ragdoll tracks an animated target rather than generating gait from
torques alone.
