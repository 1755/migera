# 8.2.2 The generalized coordinates and degrees of freedom

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §8.2.2, pp. 205–207 ·
> [open PDF at p. 205](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=218) ·
> Up: [8.2 Mathematical formulation](./INDEX.md)

Any set of time-dependent parameters that fixes the configuration without
ambiguity can serve as **generalized coordinates** $[q]$. The degrees of
freedom are counted as $6S + 3P - C$. Every constraint equation brings a
constraint force, its **Lagrange multiplier**, which plays the role of a
reaction force. If you use only independent coordinates (as many as there
are DOF), you never have to solve for those forces.

## Key ideas

- **Generalized coordinates** $[q]^t = [q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n]$ (Eq. 8.1;
  $[\,]^t$ means transpose). A point $p$ in the plane can be located by
  $(x,y)$, $(r,\theta)$, $(r,\sin\theta)$, and so on (Fig. 8.1). With
  $q_1 = r$ and $q_2 = \sin\theta$:
  $x = q_1\sqrt{1 - q_2^2}$ and $y = q_1 q_2$.
- **DOF.** A spatial rigid segment has at most 6 DOF and a point mass at
  most 3. A system's DOF is the sum over its elements minus its constraints.
- **Constraints** are equations $[\Phi] = [0]$. They come from joints (a pin
  joint makes two points on adjacent segments share one trajectory) or from
  an externally imposed motion. They enter the equations of motion as
  **constraint forces** $[\lambda]^t$, one per constraint equation. For the
  planar particle, $\Phi$ is $z = 0$, and $\lambda$ is the $z$-force that
  holds the particle in the plane.
- **DOF = 0** (totally constrained): solve it kinematically.
  **DOF < 0** (overconstrained): it cannot be solved until the redundant
  constraints are removed.
- **Independent coordinates** can be varied freely without violating any
  constraint, and there are exactly as many as there are DOF. Extra
  coordinates are **superfluous (dependent)**. The relations between the
  two sets are themselves constraint equations.
- **Holonomic:** the dependent coordinates can be eliminated.
  **Nonholonomic** systems always need more coordinates than they have DOF.
- **Generalized velocity** $\dot q_i$ and **generalized acceleration**
  $\ddot q_i$.

## Equations and numbers

$$\text{DOF} = 6S + 3P - C \tag{8.2}$$

with $S$ segments, $P$ particles and $C$ constraint equations.

Transformation equations (Eq. 8.3), mapping coordinates to the Cartesian
position of point $i$:

$$x_i = f_{xi}(q_1,\dots,q_n,t),\quad y_i = f_{yi}(\dots),\quad z_i = f_{zi}(\dots)$$

Velocity by the chain rule (Eq. 8.4). The printed equation has
$\partial q_i/\partial t$ inside the sum. The correct term is
$\dot q_j = dq_j/dt$:

$$\dot x_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial q_j}\,\dot q_j + \frac{\partial x_i}{\partial t}$$

The $\dot y_i$ and $\dot z_i$ components follow the same pattern.

## Relevance to migera

- **Counting the ragdoll.** migera's ragdoll has 14 bodies
  (`ragdoll_plugin.rs`: pelvis, two torso, head, four arm, six leg). They
  form a tree joined by 13 `SphericalJoint`s, each removing 3 translational
  DOF. With a free root the count is $6\cdot14 - 3\cdot13 = 45$ DOF. With
  a pinned kinematic root it is $3\cdot13 = 39$ rotational DOF, one ball
  joint's worth per non-root body. This matches one PD controller per joint.
- **Maximal vs. reduced coordinates.** avian keeps every body's full 6 DOF
  (maximal coordinates) and enforces the joints as constraint equations.
  That is the "superfluous coordinates + constraints + multipliers" route
  of this section. A Featherstone-style articulated solver uses the
  independent coordinates instead. The book's trade-off holds in both
  cases: multipliers give you the joint reaction forces, and reduced
  coordinates give fewer equations.
- Joint limits are *inequality* constraints, which Winter's holonomic
  $[\Phi]=0$ framework does not cover. The book models them as springs
  instead (§8.1).
