# 4.1.3 Segment mass and center of mass

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §4.1.3, pp. 85–88 ·
> [open PDF at p. 85](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=98) ·
> Up: [4.1 Density, mass, and inertial properties](./INDEX.md)

Each segment's mass is a fixed fraction of body mass, and its center of mass
(COM) sits at a fixed fraction of its length from the proximal joint.
Table 4.1 — Dempster's cadaver data compiled by Winter and used throughout
the book — gives those fractions, plus radii of gyration and densities, for
every segment and common segment groupings.

Contents: [Key ideas](#key-ideas) · [Table 4.1](#table-41-anthropometric-data) ·
[Equations](#equations-and-numbers) · [migera mapping](#relevance-to-migera)

## Key ideas

- **Center of mass vs center of gravity:** COM is the general term; center
  of gravity is the COM along the gravity axis only. In the horizontal axes
  only "COM" is correct.
- Segment mass scales with body mass, so it is tabulated as a fraction.
- COM is tabulated as a fraction of segment length from the proximal *or*
  distal end (they sum to 1). Segment endpoints are **anatomical landmarks**
  (the "Definition" column), not rig joints; match the definition before
  using a row.
- In cadavers the COM is the balance point; in vivo it needs the
  cross-sectional-area profile along the segment (Eqs. 4.3–4.5).
- "Leg" in Winter means the **shank** (knee→ankle), not the whole lower limb.

Reading notes (not in the book, but follow from it):

- **Direction of "proximal" for the trunk rows** is the first-named landmark
  (thorax: C7–T1 is proximal, so its COM is 0.82 of the way *down* toward
  T12–L1; pelvis COM is 0.105 from L4–L5). Combined with the footnote, the
  trunk-part COM fractions are ambiguous about which length they multiply;
  measure against a real torso before trusting them to better than a few cm.
- **Head and neck:** COM fraction 1.000 from C7–T1 means the COM is *at*
  the ear canal. ρ about C7–T1 is printed **0.116**, which is impossible
  because it is smaller than ρ about COM (0.495). The parallel-axis theorem
  gives √(0.495² + 1.000²) = **1.116**, so the printed value has lost its
  leading "1". Use 1.116.
- **Parallel-axis self-check** (ρ_prox² = ρ₀² + f_prox², ρ_dist² = ρ₀² +
  f_dist²): every other row reproduces its ρ_prox and ρ_dist within 0.004.
  The exception is total leg, printed 0.560/0.650, which computes to
  0.553/0.642. So the ρ₀ and COM columns are the trustworthy ones, and the
  end-radii can be derived from them.
- **Last HAT row** uses a short segment (trochanter→mid rib) so its COM lies
  *beyond* the distal marker (1.142 L) — Example 4.7 uses this row.
- Mass check: head-neck 0.081 + thorax 0.216 + abdomen 0.139 + pelvis
  0.142 + 2×(upper arm 0.028 + forearm-and-hand 0.022 + thigh 0.100 + leg
  0.0465 + foot 0.0145) = **1.000**. Hand 0.006 + forearm 0.016 =
  forearm-and-hand 0.022; foot + leg = 0.061; thigh + 0.061 = total leg 0.161;
  thorax + abdomen + pelvis = trunk 0.497; trunk + head-neck = 0.578;
  + 2 × 0.050 arms = HAT 0.678. The table is internally consistent.
- The ρ values are for rotation in the plane of the book's 2D analyses
  (about a transverse axis). The table has **no longitudinal (twist-axis)
  inertia**.

## Equations and numbers

Segment split into *n* slices of mass mᵢ, density dᵢ, volume Vᵢ, at
distance xᵢ from the left end (Fig. 4.3):

$$M = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \tag{4.3}$$

$$m_i = d_i V_i;\quad \text{uniform density: } M = d \sum_{i=1}^{n} V_i \tag{4.4}$$

The COM reproduces the distributed mass's gravitational moment about any
point on the axis:

$$M\bar{x} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i x_i \quad\Rightarrow\quad \bar{x} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i x_i \tag{4.5}$$

COM of a segment from its end markers P (proximal) and D (distal):
**COM = P + f·(D − P)** with *f* = "COM from proximal".

Example 4.2 (p. 87–88), coordinates in cm: ankle (84.9, 11.0), metatarsal
(101.1, 1.3) → foot COM (93.0, 6.15). Greater trochanter (72.1, 92.8),
lateral femoral condyle (86.4, 54.9) → thigh COM = trochanter + 0.433·(condyle
− trochanter) = (78.3, 76.4).

## Relevance to migera

Directly load-bearing for the 14-body active ragdoll. Verified in
`src/character/anim/ragdoll_plugin.rs` (2026-09-28):

- **Masses already come from this table.** `default_body_masses()` gives
  a 70 kg body: Hips 0.142 (pelvis), Spine 0.139 (abdomen), Spine2 0.216
  (thorax), Head 0.081 (head and neck), upper arm 0.028, forearm
  0.022 (forearm *and hand*), UpLeg 0.100 (thigh), Leg 0.0465 (shank),
  Foot 0.0145. These sum to exactly 1.000, as checked above. `density_for`
  back-solves a capsule density so avian's `ComputedMass` hits each mass.
  Its doc comment says only the ratios matter, because the PD controller is
  acceleration-shaped.
- **The COM is not from the table.** Each body is a capsule centred on its
  segment midpoint, so its COM is at 0.5 L. Table 4.1 puts it at 0.433 L for
  the thigh and shank, 0.436 L for the upper arm, and **0.682 L** for the
  forearm-and-hand. The forearm-and-hand is the only large error: the hand's
  mass sits beyond the wrist joint that ends the capsule.
- **The inertia is not from the table.** avian derives it from a
  uniform-density capsule with radius 0.22 L, capped at 0.09 m. For
  r/L = 0.2 that gives ρ_COM ≈ 0.38 L (0.39 at r/L = 0.22; computed from the
  capsule inertia formula), against the table's 0.302–0.323 L for thigh,
  shank and upper arm. That is roughly 45–65% too much transverse inertia
  for those segments. For the forearm-and-hand the table's ρ = 0.468 L is
  about the capsule's value, but it is taken about a different COM.
- **How to apply it:** avian 0.7 has explicit `CenterOfMass` and
  `AngularInertia` components (`avian3d-0.7.0/src/dynamics/rigid_body/mass_properties/components/mod.rs`).
  The ragdoll does not use them today. Setting COM = f·L from the proximal
  joint, expressed in the body's midpoint frame, and I⊥ = m(ρ₀L)² per body
  would make the bodies anthropometric. Use about 0.1–0.2 of I⊥ for the
  long axis; the book gives none. This matters for how a hit
  (`RagdollHit::at_point`) splits into spin versus travel, and for how
  joint constraints share corrections. It does not matter for PD tracking
  itself, because that is acceleration-shaped.
- For whole-body COM (balance, pelvis sway): sum fᵢ·COMᵢ over the same
  segments ([4.1.7](./4.1.7-anthropometric-tables-with-kinematic-data.md)).
