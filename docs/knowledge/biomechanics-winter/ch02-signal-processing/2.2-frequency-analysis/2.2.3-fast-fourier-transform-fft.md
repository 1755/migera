# 2.2.3 Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §2.2.3, p. 30 ·
> [open PDF at p. 30](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=43) ·
> Up: [2.2 Frequency analysis](./INDEX.md)

The FFT computes the same coefficients as the DFT in N log₂N instead of N²
operations by recursively halving the record into even and odd samples and
reusing one table of sines and cosines — at the price of a power-of-two
record length.

## Key ideas

- **Danielson–Lanczos lemma (1942):** a length-N DFT splits into two
  length-N/2 DFTs (even- and odd-indexed samples), recursively down to
  length 1 — hence the binary (power-of-two) record length.
- **Cooley & Tukey (IBM, mid-1960s)** produced the best-known algorithm;
  many variants followed (Brigham, 1974).
- **Where the savings come from:** harmonic n reuses every n-th entry of
  the fundamental's sine/cosine table, and products sharing the same sine
  value are summed first and multiplied once.
- **Record length:** an FFT of this classic kind uses only the largest
  power-of-two block of the data. 1000 samples → 512 used, 488 wasted; plan
  collection at 1024.

## Equations and numbers

| | Operations | N = 4096 at 0.1 µs each |
|---|---|---|
| DFT | $N^2$ | 4096² × 10⁻⁷ s = 1.67 s |
| FFT | $N\log_2 N$ | 4096·12 × 10⁻⁷ s ≈ 4.9 ms (book prints "49 ms") |

**Arithmetic check:** 4096 × 12 × 10⁻⁷ = 4.9 × 10⁻³ s, so the book's
"49 ms" is off by 10×; the ratio N/log₂N = 341 is the real point.

## Relevance to migera

Low for runtime code — procedural gait analysis in tests deals with tens to
hundreds of samples per stride, where a direct DFT (Eq. 2.13–2.14) is
trivial and avoids the power-of-two constraint. Modern FFT libraries
(e.g. mixed-radix) do not waste non-power-of-two data. Useful only if long
stochastic signals (ragdoll sway, layered idle over minutes) are analysed.
