# 2.2.4 Applications of spectrum analyses

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §2.2.4, pp. 30–41 ·
> [open PDF at p. 30](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=43) ·
> Up: [2.2 Frequency analysis](./INDEX.md)

Knowing a signal's spectrum settles how fast to sample it (at least twice
its highest frequency), how long to record it (long enough for its lowest
frequency), and where to cut off noise. For normal walking the numbers are
concrete: the signal lives in the first ~7 stride harmonics (below ~6 Hz),
so 25 Hz sampling and a 6 Hz low-pass are adequate — but even 9 harmonics
cannot faithfully rebuild a foot trajectory, because its frequency content
changes between stance and swing.

## Key ideas

**2.2.4.1 Analog-to-digital conversion (Fig. 2.11).** Sample-and-hold turns
the analog signal into pulses, which are coded into binary words. A 12-bit
converter gives 2¹² = 4096 levels; 5 s at 100 samples/s = 500 words.

**2.2.4.2 Sampling rate — the sampling theorem.** Sample at no less than
twice the highest frequency present, or **aliasing** creates false
frequencies that were never in the signal (Fig. 2.12: a signal sampled less
than twice per cycle yields the same samples as a much slower one). For
normal and pathological gait, 24 fps film suffices for kinetic and energy
analyses (Winter, 1982): foot forward acceleration recomputed from every
second frame of 50 Hz data (i.e. 25 Hz) differs visibly only at the
negative acceleration peak (Fig. 2.13), and the hip moment barely changes
(Fig. 2.14) because joint reaction forces dominate the inertial terms.
Fast running and athletic movements need more.

**2.2.4.3 Record length.** Set by the lowest frequency. For a cyclic
movement that is the stride frequency: 105 steps/min = 1.75 steps/s =
0.875 strides/s → fundamental 0.875 Hz. Non-cyclic tasks have no defined
lowest frequency: in quiet standing (inverted-pendulum model, Gage et al.,
2004) the A/P COP and COM spectra (Fig. 2.15; 137 s, 8192 samples at 60 Hz,
resolution 0.0073 Hz) are dominated by components below 0.2 Hz — mean power
frequency COPx 0.124 Hz, COMx 0.0565 Hz — so records of **at least one
minute** are needed (Carpenter et al., 2001).

**2.2.4.4 Filtering — noise and movement artifacts.**
- Signal occupies the low end of the spectrum and overlaps higher-frequency
  noise (Fig. 2.16a). A low-pass filter passes below cutoff $f_c$ with gain
  1 and attenuates above (2.16b); output spectrum = input × response (2.16c).
- Trade-off: $f_c$ too high passes noise; too low distorts the signal near
  $f_c$. Sharper filters cost more computation.
- **Gait spectrum (Winter et al., 1974; 20 subjects, Fig. 2.17):** the
  vertical toe-marker trajectory — the highest-frequency marker along with
  the heel — has **99.7% of its signal power in the lowest seven harmonics
  (below 6 Hz)**. Above that, content up to the 20th harmonic analysed looks
  like random noise (digitizing, electronic, spatial precision). Hence a
  **6 Hz cutoff for walking kinematics**. This noise matters most when
  differentiating to velocity and acceleration (§3.4.3).
- The recursive (IIR) 2nd-order filter, its coefficient formulas, and a
  worked example are below; phase-shift removal by multiple passes is in
  §3.4.4.2.

**2.2.4.5 Fourier reconstitution (Fig. 2.18).** One stride of a toe
marker's vertical trajectory rebuilt from its first nine harmonics (more
did not help) is *visibly different* from the original — enough to cause
errors in later analysis. Cause: each harmonic's amplitude and phase are
averages over the stride (§2.2.2), but a foot marker is non-stationary —
high frequencies in swing, low in stance.

**2.2.4.6 Fourier analysis of white noise (Fig. 2.19).** Uniform random
±1 at 2048 samples/s for 1 s; FFT at 1 Hz spacing from 0 to the 1024 Hz
Nyquist limit. The raw spectrum looks noisy; a 40-point moving average is
roughly flat (mean 0.330, range 0.233–0.446), approaching the theoretical
constant; a longer record flattens it further. Some individual harmonic
amplitudes exceed 1 even though the signal is ±1, because the harmonics'
differing phases cancel when summed.

## Equations and numbers

Sampling: $f_s \ge 2 f_{max}$; Nyquist frequency $f_s/2$.

Recursive 2nd-order low-pass filter (X raw, $X^1$ filtered, T the sample
interval):

$$
X^1(nT) = a_0X(nT) + a_1X(nT-T) + a_2X(nT-2T) + b_1X^1(nT-T) + b_2X^1(nT-2T)
\qquad (2.18)
$$

Coefficients:

$$
\omega_c = \frac{\tan(\pi f_c/f_s)}{C} \qquad (2.19)
$$

- $C$ = correction for the number of passes (C = 1 for a single pass;
  multi-pass values in §3.4.4.2).
- $K_1 = \sqrt2\,\omega_c$ (Butterworth) or $2\omega_c$ (critically damped).
- $K_2 = \omega_c^2$, $\;a_0 = \dfrac{K_2}{1+K_1+K_2}$, $\;a_1 = 2a_0$, $\;a_2 = a_0$.
- $K_3 = \dfrac{2a_0}{K_2}$, $\;b_1 = -2a_0 + K_3$.
- $b_2 = 1 - 2a_0 - K_3$ (equivalently $1 - a_0 - a_1 - a_2 - b_1$).

Only the ratio $f_s/f_c$ matters. Worked example, Butterworth, $f_s/f_c$ =
60/6 = 10: $a_0 = 0.067455$, $a_1 = 0.13491$, $a_2 = 0.067455$,
$b_1 = 1.14298$, $b_2 = -0.41280$; the coefficients sum to 1 (unity gain
in the passband). *Checked:* evaluating the formulas above in Python
reproduces these five values to all printed digits. The critically damped
variant at the same ratio gives $a_0 = 0.060141$, $b_1 = 1.019051$,
$b_2 = -0.259616$ (computed, not in the book). The text's reference to
"Equation (2.17)" for C means Eq. 2.19.

Other numbers:

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Walking fundamental at 105 steps/min | 0.875 Hz |
| Toe-marker power in harmonics 1–7 | 99.7% (below 6 Hz) |
| Kinematic cutoff for walking | ≈ 6 Hz |
| Adequate sampling for walking | 24–25 fps |
| Quiet-standing mean power freq. | COPx 0.124 Hz, COMx 0.0565 Hz |
| Quiet-standing minimum record | ≥ 1 min |
| Harmonics used in Fig. 2.18 reconstitution | 9 (still visibly wrong) |

Fig. 2.17 approximate mean normalized harmonic amplitudes (fundamental =
1.00, read off the log plot, ±~20%): harmonics 2–4 ≈ 0.5–0.6, 5 ≈ 0.2,
6–7 ≈ 0.1, 8 ≈ 0.05, 9–11 ≈ 0.03, 12–20 ≈ 0.01–0.02. The toe's vertical
path is strongly non-sinusoidal: harmonics 2–4 are each about half the
fundamental.

## Relevance to migera

- **How many harmonics a procedural gait needs.** Fig. 2.17 is the answer
  for the hardest case (the toe): about 7 harmonics of stride frequency
  hold 99.7% of the power. Joint angles are smoother than a toe marker, so
  a per-joint Fourier series of ≤ 7 terms is a sound compact representation
  — and a single `PhaseOscillator` term (`phase.rs`) is demonstrably too
  few for any leg DOF, consistent with `gait.rs`'s reasons for not using
  one.
- **But don't Fourier-encode foot trajectories.** §2.2.4.5 shows 9
  harmonics still misrepresent a foot's vertical path because stance is
  flat and swing is fast. That is the same stance/swing asymmetry
  `gait.rs` handles with piecewise curves and foot locking (`footlock.rs`)
  handles by pinning the foot — keep feet phase-segmented, and use harmonics
  for joint angles or for *analysing* output, not for synthesizing contact.
- **Test sampling.** 25 samples/s — about 25–30 per stride — is enough to
  capture walking kinematics; a test that samples a procedural stride at
  ≥ 50 points has headroom. Accelerations are far more sensitive
  (Fig. 2.13), so derivative-based assertions need denser sampling.
- **Ragdoll/physics sampling.** At avian's 64 Hz step, the Nyquist limit is
  32 Hz, well above gait content (< 6 Hz); motion above that (PD chatter)
  aliases rather than disappears — read ragdoll traces with that in mind.
- **Critically damped filter = critically damped spring.** The
  critically damped variant of Eq. 2.18 is a discrete 2nd-order low-pass
  with no overshoot, and a `dho.rs` joint spring with `SpringParams::critical`
  chasing its target pose is the same thing in continuous time — a
  critically damped 2nd-order low-pass on the target signal. Its coefficient set is a ready test oracle for any
  discretized smoothing filter; see also the exp-approximation lesson
  before "optimizing" such code.
- **Low relevance:** ADC hardware, camera cost arguments.
