# 2.1.5 Importance in removing the mean bias from the signal

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §2.1.5, pp. 21–22 ·
> [open PDF at p. 21](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=34) ·
> Up: [2.1 Auto- and cross-correlation analyses](./INDEX.md)

If either signal keeps its mean (dc bias), the cross-correlation gains a
constant term equal to the product of the means, which swamps the real
correlation and inflates its peak. Standard programs remove means silently;
your own code must do it explicitly.

## Key ideas

- Write $x = s_1 + m_1$, $y = s_2 + m_2$ with $s_1, s_2$ zero-mean.
- Expanding the product gives four integrals; the two mixed ones
  ($m_1\int s_2$ and $m_2\int s_1$) vanish because $s_1, s_2$ have zero
  mean over the record.
- The survivor $\int_0^T m_1 m_2\,dt$ is a τ-independent offset added to the
  wanted correlation — "a major error".

## Equations and numbers

$$
R_{xy}(\tau) = \int_0^T s_1(t)\,s_2(t+\tau)\,dt + \int_0^T m_1 m_2\,dt
$$

(numerators only). The first term is the desired correlation; the second is
the bias.

## Where to read in the book

- pp. 21–22 (PDF 34–35): the expansion and the conclusion.

## Relevance to migera

Joint-angle curves from a procedural walk carry big offsets (knee
flexion is one-signed and never averages to zero; a thigh swings about a
nonzero mean), so a
hand-written cross-correlation in a Rust test that forgets mean removal
will report high correlation for almost any pair of curves — a test that
cannot fail. Subtract each curve's mean over the analysed window before
correlating, and prove the test can fail by feeding it a deliberately
phase-shifted or inverted curve.
