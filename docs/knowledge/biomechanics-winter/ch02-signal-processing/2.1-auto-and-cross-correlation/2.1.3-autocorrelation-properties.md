# 2.1.3 Four properties of the autocorrelation function

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §2.1.3, pp. 17–20 ·
> [open PDF at p. 17](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=30) ·
> Up: [2.1 Auto- and cross-correlation analyses](./INDEX.md)

The autocorrelation is maximal at zero shift, symmetric in τ, repeats with
the period of any periodic component (while discarding that component's
phase), and is additive over uncorrelated parts — so a periodic signal
buried in white noise shows up as a clean cosine riding on a spike at τ = 0.

## Key ideas

1. **Maximum at τ = 0.** $R_{xx}(0)$ is the mean square of x; no shift can
   correlate better than no shift.
2. **Even function.** $R_{xx}(\tau) = R_{xx}(-\tau)$, so only positive
   shifts need computing.
3. **Periodic in, periodic out — phase lost.** A sine and a cosine of the
   same frequency have the *same* autocorrelation, a cosine in τ (Fig. 2.3).
   Useful for finding periodic signals buried in noise.
   - **White noise**: every sample uncorrelated with every other, so
     $R_{xx}(\tau) = 0$ for τ ≠ 0 and 1 at τ = 0 — an impulse (Fig. 2.4).
   - Signal + uncorrelated noise: the cross terms vanish and the
     autocorrelations add (Eq. 2.7).
4. **Spectrum inside.** The power spectral density is the Fourier transform
   of $R_{xx}(\tau)$. Even without periodicity, the *width* of $R_{xx}$
   indicates frequency content: low-frequency signals stay correlated for
   long shifts, high-frequency ones decorrelate quickly.

## Equations and numbers

Property 1, from $\int_0^T (x(t)-x(t-\tau))^2 dt \ge 0$ expanded (the two
squared terms are equal for a fixed τ):

$$
R_{xx}(0) + R_{xx}(0) - 2R_{xx}(\tau) \ge 0 \;\Rightarrow\; R_{xx}(0) - R_{xx}(\tau) \ge 0
\qquad (2.4)
$$

Property 2, substituting $t = t' - \tau$, $dt = dt'$:

$$
R_{xx}(\tau) = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t'-\tau)\,x(t')\,dt' = R_{xx}(-\tau)
\qquad (2.5)
$$

Property 3, for $x(t) = E\sin(\omega t)$ over one period T, using
$\sin a \sin b = \tfrac12[\cos(a-b) - \cos(a+b)]$ (numerator only):

$$
R_{xx}(\tau) = \frac{E^2}{2}\cos(\omega\tau)
\qquad (2.6)
$$

and identically for $x(t) = E\cos(\omega t)$. Normalized by
$R_{xx}(0) = E^2/2$ it is simply $\cos(\omega\tau)$; its first zero crossing
is at a quarter period (used in [2.1.7](./2.1.7-autocorrelation-applications.md)).

Signal plus uncorrelated noise, $x = s + n$:

$$
R_{xx}(\tau) = R_{ss}(\tau) + R_{nn}(\tau)
\qquad (2.7)
$$

**Transcription note.** The printed intermediate steps on pp. 18–19 swap t
and τ in places (e.g. "t cos(ωt)" and "sin(ωt)" where $\cos(\omega\tau)$
and $\sin(\omega\tau)$ are meant); the final Eq. 2.6 is correct.

## Relevance to migera

- **Test oracle.** Eq. 2.6 is an exact expectation: the normalized circular
  autocorrelation of a pure `PhaseOscillator` curve (`phase.rs`) must equal
  $\cos(\omega\tau)$. A deviation means the sampled curve is not the sinusoid
  the code claims.
- **Checking "never repeats".** `phase.rs` layers oscillators with
  deliberately non-commensurate periods so an idle never visibly loops. The
  autocorrelation of the summed curve over a long window should have no
  secondary peak near 1 at any nonzero lag; a peak says the layer does
  repeat, and at which lag.
- **Stride period from data.** The first nonzero-lag peak of a joint angle's
  autocorrelation is the stride period — a phase-free way to measure the
  cadence a procedural walk actually produces.
