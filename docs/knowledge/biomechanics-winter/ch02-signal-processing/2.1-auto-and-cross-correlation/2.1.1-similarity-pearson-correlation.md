# 2.1.1 Similarity to the Pearson correlation

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §2.1.1, pp. 15–16 ·
> [open PDF at p. 15](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=28) ·
> Up: [2.1 Auto- and cross-correlation analyses](./INDEX.md)

The Pearson coefficient is the mean product of two mean-removed variables,
normalized by both standard deviations so it is unitless and bounded to
[−1, +1]. Auto- and cross-correlation are the same computation repeated at
each time shift of one series against the other.

## Key ideas

- **Sign and magnitude.** Positive r: x and y rise and fall together;
  negative: one rises as the other falls; |r| is the strength.
- **Why it works (Figs. 2.1, 2.2).** With means removed, unrelated data
  scatter evenly over the four quadrants, so the products (+ in quadrants 1
  and 3, − in 2 and 4) cancel toward zero. Related data lie along a sloped
  line and the products accumulate one sign.
- **Each normalization has a job.** Dividing by N removes dependence on the
  number of points; dividing by $s_x s_y$ removes the units and the scale of
  the variables.
- **Finite-sample error.** With few points r is an estimate; its
  significance depends on N (see any statistics table for r).

## Equations and numbers

$$
r = \frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{s_x\, s_y}
\qquad (2.1)
$$

$x_i, y_i$: the *i*-th samples; $\bar x, \bar y$: means; $s_x, s_y$:
standard deviations (population form, matching the $1/N$).

## Relevance to migera

Directly usable as a shape-similarity score between a procedural joint-angle
curve and a reference curve (e.g. Appendix A) sampled at the same stride
percentages: r ≈ 1 says "same shape" independent of amplitude and offset.
That independence is also its trap — r is blind to a wrong amplitude or a
constant offset, so a test that only checks r can pass a walk with half the
real knee flexion. Pair it with an amplitude or RMS check.
