# 2.1.7 Application of autocorrelations

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §2.1.7, p. 23 (Fig. 2.6 on p. 24) ·
> [open PDF at p. 23](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=36) ·
> Up: [2.1 Auto- and cross-correlation analyses](./INDEX.md)

An autocorrelation exposes periodic content that is invisible in the raw
trace, and its first zero crossing gives a quick estimate of the dominant
frequency.

## Key ideas

- **Hidden interference.** A 0.5 s surface EMG record (Fig. 2.6a) shows no
  visible hum, but its autocorrelation over τ = ±100 ms (Fig. 2.6b) is even
  and carries a sinusoidal ripple with peaks ~17 ms apart — 60 Hz mains hum.
- **Zero-crossing frequency estimate.** A sinusoid's normalized
  autocorrelation $\cos(\omega\tau)$ first crosses zero at a quarter period.
  So the first zero crossing $\tau_0$ gives an average period $4\tau_0$.

## Equations and numbers

$$
f_{avg} \approx \frac{1}{4\,\tau_0}
$$

EMG example: $\tau_0 \approx 3$ ms → period ≈ 12 ms → $f_{avg} \approx$ 83 Hz.
Hum period ≈ 17 ms ↔ 60 Hz.

## Where to read in the book

- p. 23 (PDF 36): the method.
- p. 24 (PDF 37): Fig. 2.6 — raw EMG and its autocorrelation.

## Relevance to migera

Moderate. The quarter-period rule is a cheap, FFT-free way to check the
dominant frequency of a sampled procedural signal — e.g. that a head bob
really runs at twice stride frequency (the property `phase.rs` currently
tests by counting zero crossings). Hum detection itself is not relevant.
