# 10.2.4 Common-mode rejection

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §10.2.4, pp. 261–265 ·
> [open PDF at p. 261](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=274) ·
> Up: [10.2 Recording of the electromyogram](./INDEX.md)

The body is an antenna for mains radiation, and the resulting hum (up to
~100 mV) dwarfs the EMG. Because the hum is nearly identical at both
electrodes, a differential amplifier subtracts it away; the common-mode
rejection ratio (CMRR) measures how well.

## Key ideas

- A single-ended amplifier passes the hum along with the EMG (Fig. 10.9).
- A differential amplifier amplifies the difference between its two active
  inputs; a signal present equally on both is a **common-mode signal** and
  cancels, while the two electrodes' EMGs differ and do not (Fig. 10.10).
- Subtraction is never perfect; CMRR = fraction of the common signal
  rejected. Residual hum is visible on the baseline when no EMG is present
  (Fig. 10.11). Only battery equipment away from mains is hum-free.

## Equations and numbers

$$e_o = A(e_1 - e_2) = A(V_{hum} + emg_1 - V_{hum} - emg_2) = A(emg_1 - emg_2) \qquad (10.4)$$

$$V_o(\text{hum}) = \frac{A\,V_{hum}}{\text{CMRR}} \qquad (10.5)
\qquad \text{CMRR (dB)} = 20\log_{10}\text{CMRR(linear)} \qquad (10.6)$$

**Example 10.1:** EMG 2 mV, hum 500 mV, CMRR 10,000:1, gain 2000 →
output EMG = 2000 × 2 mV = 4 V (printed "2 MV", a typo); output hum =
2000 × 500 mV / 10,000 = 100 mV.

CMRR 10,000:1 = 80 dB; good bioamplifiers should reach ≥ 80 dB.
