# 10.4.1 Electromyogram versus isometric tension

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §10.4.1, pp. 273–275 ·
> [open PDF at p. 273](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=286) ·
> Up: [10.4 Relationship between electromyogram and biomechanical variables](./INDEX.md)

In isometric contractions EMG amplitude rises with tension, but the shape of
that curve is muscle- and length-dependent: linear for some muscles,
strongly nonlinear (EMG rising faster than force) for others. Under changing
tension, force lags the EMG by the twitch delay. A calibrated linear envelope
is therefore only a **coarse** tension predictor, and only while muscle
length is not changing rapidly.

## Key ideas

- For constant tension, the mean of the rectified EMG (a very long
  time-constant envelope) suffices; for time-varying tension use the linear
  envelope (Bouisset 1973 review).
- **Linear:** Lippold (1952), human calf muscles (Fig. 10.17 left, two
  trials $t_1$, $t_2$).
- **Nonlinear:** Zuniga & Simons (1969) and Vredenbregt & Rau (1973), elbow
  flexors over a range of joint angles. Fig. 10.17 right: elbow-flexor EMG vs
  wrist force at 56°, 138° and 162°; each curve is concave-up (EMG grows
  faster than force at high effort) and the curves differ substantially with
  angle. These are static calibrations at fixed lengths.
- Motor-unit **count rate** tracks integrated EMG linearly (Close et al. 1960),
  hence also rises almost linearly with tension.
- **Dynamics:** Inman et al. (1952) matched force-transducer records with the
  envelope EMG; Gottlieb & Agarwal (1971) modelled the relation as a
  second-order low-pass. Tension lags EMG because each twitch peaks
  40–100 ms after its m.u.a.p. (Fig. 10.18: tension starts after EMG onset,
  and persists after EMG stops, both for a slow 2 s build-up with rapid
  relaxation and for a short 400 ms burst).
- Open problems for dynamic, multi-muscle use: (1) does length change only
  alter mechanical advantage, or the EMG itself? (2) how do synergists with
  multiple functions share load? (3) antagonist co-activation adds an
  unknown opposing force.

## Equations and numbers

- Twitch delay behind EMG: peak 40–100 ms after the m.u.a.p. (§10.3.2 quotes
  50–110 ms for the peak).
- Fig. 10.17 right axes: EMG 0–1.5+ mV vs wrist force 0–400 N (read from the
  figure; approximate).

## Relevance to migera

- **Don't assume activation → torque is linear across a joint's range.**
  migera's ragdoll strength scales a torque ceiling linearly. The book shows
  the real relation depends on the muscle and on joint angle. Linear is a
  defensible first choice (Lippold's calf data), but a per-joint,
  angle-dependent gain curve is the physiologically honest refinement. Not
  built; a design lead.
- **Lag is real and measured.** Fig. 10.18 is the empirical justification
  for filtering strength changes with the twitch-time low-pass of
  [10.3.2](../10.3-processing-the-emg/10.3.2-linear-envelope.md): force should
  rise and fall 40–100 ms behind a strength command, not on the same frame.
- **Co-contraction** (open question 3) maps onto stiffening a joint without
  moving it; the book offers no model, only the warning that EMG of one
  muscle cannot tell you net joint torque.
