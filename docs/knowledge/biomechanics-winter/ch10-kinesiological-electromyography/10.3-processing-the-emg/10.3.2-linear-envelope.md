# 10.3.2 Linear envelope

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §10.3.2, pp. 271–272 ·
> [open PDF at p. 271](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=284) ·
> Up: [10.3 Processing of the electromyogram](./INDEX.md)

Low-pass filtering the full-wave-rectified EMG gives the **linear envelope**,
a moving average that closely resembles the muscle's tension curve. Winter's
point is that the filter should not be arbitrary: treat each rectified
m.u.a.p. as an impulse and the twitch as its response, and the right filter is
a **critically damped second-order low-pass** whose impulse response *is* the
twitch. Cutoff follows from twitch time: $f_c = 1/(2\pi T)$, 1.5–4 Hz.

## Key ideas

- **Name.** It is a linear envelope, reported in mV. Calling it "integrated
  EMG (IEMG)" is wrong; true integration is a different operation
  ([10.3.3](./10.3.3-true-mathematical-integrators.md)).
- **Why a physiological basis matters.** Rectify-and-filter at a high,
  arbitrary cutoff (Forssberg 1985; Murray et al. 1985) produces a smooth
  curve with no relation to force. If the envelope should track muscle force
  or joint moment, the processing must model tension generation.
- **Impulse/response argument.** Tension is a superposition of twitches, as
  EMG is a superposition of m.u.a.p.'s. A rectified m.u.a.p. lasts ~10 ms;
  a twitch peaks at 50–110 ms and lasts up to 300 ms. The input is short
  relative to the response, so it is effectively an impulse.
- **Twitch = critically damped (or slightly overdamped) 2nd-order system**
  (Crosby 1978; Milner-Brown et al. 1973), with cutoffs reported from
  2.3 to 7.8 Hz.
- **Equivalence to the ch. 9 model.** The EMG-driven muscle model of §9.3.1
  (Fig. 9.20) is a critically damped mass-spring-damper; a critically damped
  low-pass has *exactly* the same response. Fitting the envelope and running
  that model are the same computation at the same cutoff.
- **Evidence:** good force/envelope correlations in isometric, time-varying
  (anisotonic) contractions (Calvert & Chapman 1977; Crosby 1978).

## Equations and numbers

Cutoff vs twitch time for a critically damped 2nd-order low-pass (Eq. 10.8):

$$f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi T}$$

Its impulse response is the twitch shape of §9.0.5 (Eq. 9.1), peaking at
$t = T$:

$$h(t) \propto \frac{t}{T}\,e^{-t/T}, \qquad H(s) = \frac{1}{(1+sT)^2}$$

Fig. 10.16 table (verified against the rendered page):

| $f_c$ (Hz) | $T$ (ms) |
|---|---|
| 4 | 40 |
| 3 | 53 |
| 2 | 80 |
| 1.5 | 106 (soleus) |

**Caveat on "cutoff" (own check, not in the book):** for
$H(s)=1/(1+sT)^2$, $|H|$ at $\omega = 1/T$ is 0.5 (−6 dB), not 0.707; the
−3 dB point is at $\omega \approx 0.644/T$. So $f_c$ in Eq. 10.8 is the pole
(corner/natural) frequency of each stage, although Fig. 10.16 draws 0.707 at
$f_c$. Implement from $T$, not from a −3 dB spec, and the impulse response
will peak at $T$ as intended.

**Discrete implementation (own sketch):** two identical first-order
smoothers in cascade, each $y \mathrel{+}= \alpha(u - y)$ with
$\alpha = 1 - e^{-\Delta t/T}$; or a critically damped spring with natural
frequency $\omega = 1/T$.

## Relevance to migera

This is the chapter's most transferable result: a **muscle does not turn a
command into force instantly; it low-passes it with a critically damped
2nd-order filter whose time constant is the twitch time, 40–106 ms.**

- **Smoothing commanded ragdoll strength.** `Ragdoll::set_strength` in
  `src/character/anim/ragdoll.rs` changes a joint's torque ceiling on the
  frame it is called, and `Ragdoll::recover` ramps stun loss back linearly.
  A physiologically grounded alternative is to treat the dial as the
  *command* (the EMG) and feed the PD's torque ceiling from its linear
  envelope. Not built; this is a design lead, not a measured result.
- **Same filter migera already has.** `src/character/anim/math/spring.rs`
  uses natural frequency $w = \ln 2/\text{halflife}$ at `damping_ratio` 1.0,
  so `SpringParams::critical(halflife)` with $\text{halflife} = T \ln 2$ is
  Winter's filter: $T$ = 40, 53, 80, 106 ms ↔ halflife ≈ 0.028, 0.037,
  0.055, 0.073 s. A scalar spring on strength is the whole implementation.
- **Stability.** The 1.5–4 Hz activation band sits well below the ~10 Hz
  frequency the explicit-integration bound allows the PD itself, and
  filtering the torque ceiling does not change $k_p$/$k_d$ at all, so it
  cannot violate that bound.
- **Verification oracle.** Driving the filter with a unit impulse must peak
  at exactly $t=T$; a step input must never overshoot. Both are cheap unit
  tests independent of the implementation.
- Caution: the book's model is linear and symmetric in rise and fall; it says
  nothing about differing activation vs deactivation speeds.
