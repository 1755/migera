# 5.1 Basic link-segment equations—the free-body diagram

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §5.1, pp. 112–117 ·
> [open PDF at p. 112](../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=125) ·
> Up: [Chapter 5 — Kinetics: forces and moments of force](./INDEX.md)

Each segment is solved on its own: three planar equations (two force, one
moment about the COM) with three unknowns — the proximal joint reaction force
(x, y) and the net muscle moment at the proximal joint. Starting from the
most distal segment, whose distal loads are known (zero in swing, the ground
reaction in stance), each solution becomes the known distal load of the next
segment up the chain.

## Key ideas

- **Knowns for a segment:** COM acceleration $a_x, a_y$; segment angle
  $\theta$; angular acceleration $\alpha$; mass $m$ and moment of inertia
  about the COM $I_0$ (from anthropometric tables, Ch. 4); distal reaction
  forces $R_{xd}, R_{yd}$ and distal net moment $M_d$ (from the previous,
  more distal segment's solution).
- **Unknowns:** proximal reaction forces $R_{xp}, R_{yp}$ and proximal net
  muscle moment $M_p$.
- **Order matters:** the moment equation contains $R_{xp}, R_{yp}$ times
  their lever arms, so the two force equations must be solved first.
- **Newton's third law between segments:** the proximal-end loads found on
  segment $i$ are applied to segment $i+1$'s distal end with the *opposite*
  sign. Winter draws the distal loads already reversed (Fig. 5.5, 5.8), which
  is why they carry minus signs in the equations.
- **Sign conventions:** x positive forward (to the right, direction of
  progression), y positive up, counterclockwise moments and angular
  accelerations positive. Every unknown is assumed positive in the drawn
  direction; a negative answer means the real direction is opposite.
- **Stance terminal segment:** the foot's "distal load" is the ground
  reaction ($F_x, F_y$) acting at the centre of pressure (COP), in the
  positive sense (it pushes *on* the foot), see
  [5.2.5](./5.2-force-transducers-and-force-plates/5.2.5-combined-force-plate-and-kinematics.md).
- **Interpretation:** the net moment is the *net* of all muscles, ligaments
  and friction at that joint, never an individual muscle
  ([5.0.2](./5.0-biomechanical-models/5.0.2-forces-on-the-link-segment-model.md)).
  An acceleration is rarely caused by one "primary" force: in Example 5.3 each
  of the two reaction forces and two moments contributes substantially.

## Equations and numbers

Planar Newton–Euler for one segment (Fig. 5.5; distal loads drawn reversed):

$$\textstyle\sum F_x = m a_x:\qquad R_{xp} - R_{xd} = m a_x \tag{5.1}$$
$$\textstyle\sum F_y = m a_y:\qquad R_{yp} - R_{yd} - m g = m a_y \tag{5.2}$$
$$\textstyle\sum M_{COM} = I_0\,\alpha \tag{5.3}$$

The book leaves (5.3) in words; written out (my transcription, verified
against Example 5.3's numbers), with $\mathbf r_p, \mathbf r_d$ the vectors
from the segment COM to the proximal and distal joints and
$(\mathbf r\times\mathbf F)_z = r_x F_y - r_y F_x$:

$$M_p - M_d + (\mathbf r_p\times\mathbf R_p)_z - (\mathbf r_d\times\mathbf R_d)_z = I_0\,\alpha$$

Solved for the recursion (segment by segment, distal → proximal):

```
R_xp = m*a_x + R_xd
R_yp = m*a_y + R_yd + m*g
M_p  = I0*alpha + M_d - cross_z(r_p, R_p) + cross_z(r_d, R_d)
# then R_xd, R_yd, M_d of the next proximal segment := R_xp, R_yp, M_p
```

For a stance foot with ground force $(F_x, F_y)$ at the COP, the foot's
equations are $F_{ax}+F_x=ma_x$, $F_{ay}+F_y-mg=ma_y$, and the moment sum uses
$\mathbf r_{COP}$ with $+\mathbf F$.

**Worked examples** (usable as unit-test fixtures; $g=9.8$):

| Ex. | Segment / situation | Inputs | Results |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Foot, static one-leg stance, $m_{body}=60$ kg, $m_{foot}=0.9$ kg, GRF 4 cm anterior to ankle; foot COM 6 cm anterior to ankle | $R_{y1}=588$ N | $R_{y2}=-579.2$ N (down on foot); $M_2 - 0.02R_{y1} - 0.06R_{y2}=0 \Rightarrow M_2=-22.99$ N·m (plantarflexor) |
| 5.2 | Foot in swing, $m_{body}=80$ kg, foot 0.20 m: $m=0.0145\cdot80=1.16$ kg, $\rho_0=0.475\cdot0.20=0.095$ m, $I_0=0.0105$ kg·m² | $a_x=9.07$, $a_y=-6.62$ m/s², $\alpha=21.69$ rad/s²; ankle 9.85 cm above and 1.95 cm behind COM | $R_{x1}=10.52$ N, $R_{y1}=3.69$ N, $M_1=0.23+1.04+0.07=1.34$ N·m (dorsiflexor) |
| 5.3 | Leg (shank), same instant, 0.435 m: $m=0.0465\cdot80=3.72$ kg, $\rho_0=0.302\cdot0.435=0.131$ m, $I_0=0.0638$ kg·m² | $a_x=-0.03$, $a_y=-4.21$ m/s², $\alpha=36.9$ rad/s²; ankle at (−18.5, −16.9) cm, knee at (+14.2, +12.9) cm from COM | $R_{x2}=10.41$ N, $R_{y2}=24.48$ N, $M_2=1.34+1.78-0.68+1.34-3.48+2.35=2.65$ N·m (knee extensor) |

Example 5.3's moment equation, exactly as printed:
$M_2 - M_1 - 0.169R_{x1} + 0.185R_{y1} - 0.129R_{x2} + 0.142R_{y2} = I\alpha$.

Takeaway from 5.2: of the 1.34 N·m ankle moment, 1.04 N·m goes to
accelerating the foot's COM horizontally (via the reaction force's lever arm)
and only 0.23 N·m to its tiny rotational inertia.

## Relevance to migera

High. This recursion is the cheapest physically grounded oracle for
procedural motion: take the per-frame bone world transforms that
`src/character/anim` produces, assign Winter's segment masses/inertias
([4.1.7](../ch04-anthropometry/4.1-density-mass-inertial-properties/4.1.7-anthropometric-tables-with-kinematic-data.md)),
finite-difference COM and angle to get $a_x, a_y, \alpha$ (smoothed first — see
[3.4.3](../ch03-kinematics/3.4-processing-raw-kinematic-data/3.4.3-velocity-and-acceleration-problems.md)),
and run the recursion. Joint moments that exceed human ranges, or swing-phase
moments that change sign every frame, flag animation that no body could
produce. The three examples above are ready test fixtures with known answers.
The same net moments, divided by the distal chain's inertia about the joint,
give physically motivated magnitudes for the ragdoll's per-joint
`max_torque` ceilings, which are angular accelerations in rad/s² (see
[avian: apply angular acceleration, not torque](../../character-animation/ragdoll-and-physics/avian-apply-angular-acceleration-not-torque.md)).
Extend to 3D with [7.4.1](../ch07-three-dimensional-kinematics-and-kinetics/7.4-kinetic-analysis-reactions-and-moments/7.4.1-newton-3d-equations-of-motion.md)/[7.4.2](../ch07-three-dimensional-kinematics-and-kinetics/7.4-kinetic-analysis-reactions-and-moments/7.4.2-euler-3d-equations-of-motion.md).
