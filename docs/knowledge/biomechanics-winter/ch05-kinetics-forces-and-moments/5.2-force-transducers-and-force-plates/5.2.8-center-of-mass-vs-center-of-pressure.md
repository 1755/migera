# 5.2.8 Differences between center of mass and center of pressure

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §5.2.8, pp. 127–130 ·
> [open PDF at p. 127](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=140) ·
> Up: [5.2 Force transducers and force plates](./INDEX.md)

The COM is where the body's mass is; the COP is where the ground pushes back.
They are independent quantities, and the COP is the controller: ankle muscles
move the COP ahead of or behind the COM to accelerate the COM the other way.
To reverse the COM the COP must overshoot it, so the COP always swings wider
than the COM — calling COP "sway" is a common misuse.

## Key ideas

- **COM:** 3D weighted average of segment COMs (Section 4.1.4); its vertical
  projection to the ground (the "COG" line) is what is compared with the COP.
- **COP:** the point of application of the vertical GRF on one plate (all
  contacts on it) — the weighted average of all downward action forces. It
  depends on foot placement and on ankle muscle control, so it is the
  **neuromuscular response** to COM imbalance, not a measure of the COM.
- **The sway cycle** (Fig. 5.16), body pivoting about the ankles, foot mass
  neglected, $W = R$ constant in quiet stance; $g$ and $p$ are the horizontal
  distances of COM and COP from the ankle:
  1. COM ahead of COP: $Wg > Rp$ → clockwise (forward-falling) $\alpha$, and
     forward $\omega$.
  2. Plantarflexors increase → COP moves **ahead** of the COM: $Rp > Wg$,
     $\alpha$ reverses and starts cancelling $\omega$.
  3. $\omega$ reverses (time-integral of $\alpha$): now both counterclockwise,
     swaying backward.
  4. Plantarflexor activity reduced → COP moves **behind** the COM:
     $Wg > Rp$, $\alpha$ reverses again.
  5. $\omega$ decreases and reverses → back to state 1.
- Consequences:
  - The ankle plantar/dorsiflexors regulate the COM **by moving the COP**.
  - The COP must keep moving ahead of and behind the COM, so its range
    exceeds the COM's.
  - If the COM drifts within a few centimetres of the toes, even a COP pushed
    to the toe tips may not reverse $\omega$ → the person **must step** to
    arrest the fall.
- **Data** (Fig. 5.17, 40 s quiet stance, A/P, 50% stance width): COP and COM
  virtually in phase; COM within about ±1 cm; COP amplitude somewhat larger
  and noisier; every reversal of COM direction (high ± acceleration) coincides
  with a COP overshoot.

## Equations and numbers

Moment balance about the ankles (the qualitative core of the section; the
quantitative form is Eq. 5.9 in
[5.2.9](./5.2.9-inverted-pendulum-model.md)):

$$I\,\alpha = W g - R p \quad(\text{clockwise/forward positive; } W = R \text{ in quiet stance})$$

so the sign of the angular acceleration is the sign of (COM − COP).

## Relevance to migera

High for balance and idle motion.
- **Idle/stance sway:** a standing character's COM (≈ pelvis) should sway a
  centimetre or so A/P, slowly and smoothly, with the implied COP (under the
  feet) oscillating slightly wider and faster around it. A pelvis-sway
  generator in `src/character/anim/pelvis.rs` or a stance idle should
  therefore move the pelvis smoothly (COM is the low-pass signal), never
  jitter it.
- **Active ragdoll balance:** the controller that keeps a ragdoll upright is
  the ankle moment moving the COP; its authority is bounded by the foot
  length. When the COM projection approaches the toe or heel edge, no ankle
  torque can recover it → trigger a step (or a fall). That is a crisp,
  geometric step-trigger criterion.
- **Verification:** "COP stays inside the support polygon and leads the COM"
  is a checkable invariant for a balance controller; "COM stays inside" alone
  is not enough (see [5.2.9](./5.2.9-inverted-pendulum-model.md)).
