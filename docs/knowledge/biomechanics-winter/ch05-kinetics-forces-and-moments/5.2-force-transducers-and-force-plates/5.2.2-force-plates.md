# 5.2.2 Force plates

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §5.2.2, pp. 117–121 ·
> [open PDF at p. 117](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=130) ·
> Up: [5.2 Force transducers and force plates](./INDEX.md)

A force plate measures the 3D ground reaction force (vertical + two shear
components) and the location of its point of application, the centre of
pressure (COP). The whole-body ground reaction is nothing but the sum of
every segment's mass × COM acceleration (plus weight), so it says little about
what any single segment is doing.

## Key ideas

- The ground reaction force (GRF) has a vertical component and two shears,
  usually resolved anterior–posterior and medial–lateral. The fourth
  quantity needed for an inverse solution is the **COP**. Pressure-sensing
  shoes were expensive and vertical-only, so force plates are the standard
  (representative paper: Elftman, 1939).
- **Four-corner plate** (Fig. 5.9): triaxial load cells at (0,0), (0,Z), (X,0),
  (X,Z); the COP follows from the ratio of corner vertical forces; equal
  forces put it at the plate centre.
- **Central-pillar plate** (Fig. 5.10): one instrumented pillar; COP from the
  pillar's bending moment $M_z$ and the shear.
- **Low-load caution:** when $F_y$ is below ~2% of body weight, small errors
  in $F_y$ give large COP errors (it is in the denominator) — e.g. at heel
  contact and toe-off.
- **Typical walking record** (Fig. 5.11, central-pillar plate, normal speed):
  - $F_y$: rapid rise after heel contact to **above** body weight (body mass
    accelerated upward), dips **below** body weight in midstance as the knee
    flexes and the plate unloads, second peak above body weight at pushoff
    (plantarflexors), then falls to zero as the other limb takes the weight.
  - $F_x$ (force of plate on foot): **negative** just after heel contact —
    backward friction that stops the foot sliding forward (what fails on
    ice); goes **positive** near midstance as the plantarflexors push the
    foot back against the plate.
  - $M_z$ positive at heel contact, negative as the body weight moves forward.
  - COP starts at the heel and moves forward to the ball and toe; its
    position *relative to the foot* requires knowing where the foot is on the
    plate (from kinematics).
- **GRF is a sum of mass–acceleration products** (Eqs 5.7–5.8). Standing
  still, $F_y$ = body weight. Cancellations make it uninterpretable
  segment-by-segment: one arm accelerating up while the other accelerates
  down leaves $F_y$ unchanged.

## Equations and numbers

Four-corner plate, vertical corner forces $F_{00}, F_{X0}, F_{0Z}, F_{XZ}$,
$F_Y = F_{00}+F_{X0}+F_{0Z}+F_{XZ}$:

$$x = \frac{X}{2}\left[1 + \frac{(F_{X0}+F_{XZ}) - (F_{00}+F_{0Z})}{F_Y}\right] \tag{5.4}$$
$$z = \frac{Z}{2}\left[1 + \frac{(F_{0Z}+F_{XZ}) - (F_{00}+F_{X0})}{F_Y}\right] \tag{5.5}$$

Central-pillar plate, moments about the support axis ($M_z$ bending moment
about the pillar's rotation axis, $y_0$ distance from support axis to plate
surface, $F_x$ drawn as a reverse shear):

$$M_z - F_y\,x + F_x\,y_0 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \frac{F_x\,y_0 + M_z}{F_y} \tag{5.6}$$

Whole-body GRF for an $N$-segment body ($a_{xi}, a_{yi}$: segment-$i$ COM
accelerations):

$$F_x = \sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i\,a_{xi} \tag{5.7}$$
$$F_y = \sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i\,(a_{yi} + g) \tag{5.8}$$

## Relevance to migera

High, as a test oracle. Eqs 5.7–5.8 turn any procedural gait into an implied
ground force with no physics engine: sum body-segment masses × COM
accelerations from the animated pose (or, crudely, total mass × the hips' /
COM's acceleration). For a believable walk the vertical force should show the
double hump — above body weight around each foot's loading and pushoff, below
it in midstance — which constrains the phase of the pelvis bob in
`src/character/anim/gait.rs` / `pelvis.rs`: the COM must be *decelerating
downward / accelerating upward* (low point) around double support and at its
high point in midstance. A walk whose implied $F_x$ is not braking-then-
propulsive per step is speeding up and slowing down wrongly. Eq. 5.4–5.5 is
also the formula for a weighted COP over several contact points (e.g. the
heel/toe contacts of both feet), useful for a support-polygon balance check.
Beware the low-load caveat: a COP computed from a nearly unloaded foot is
noise.
