# 5.2.3 Special pressure-measuring sensory systems

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §5.2.3, pp. 121–122 ·
> [open PDF at p. 121](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=134) ·
> Up: [5.2 Force transducers and force plates](./INDEX.md)

The force-plate COP is a weighted average of all contact pressures — under
both feet if both are down — and says nothing about the pressure at any one
point. In midstance the load is on the heel and the ball of the foot, yet the
COP lies under the arch where the pressure may be negligible. Pressure
insoles resolve the distribution.

## Key ideas

- Tekscan-style insole (Fig. 5.12): trimmable sheet of tactile sensels,
  ~0.2 × 0.2 in (5 × 5 mm), 25 sensels per square inch; row/column electrodes
  on two polyester sheets with a pressure-dependent semiconductive layer
  between them; displayed as a colour map (blue → red, 1–125 psi).
- Shows the pressure moving heel → toes over stance; used to find high-pressure
  points in foot deformities and diabetic feet and to check surgery/orthotics
  (Hsiao et al., 2002; Pitie et al., 1999).

## Where to read in the book

- p. 121 (PDF 134): the weighted-average argument; sensel construction.
- p. 122 (PDF 135): Fig. 5.12 (insole schematic) and clinical uses.

## Relevance to migera

Low–moderate. The one useful lesson: the COP is not a contact point. A
foot-grounding or balance heuristic that snaps something to the "COP" in
midstance would put it under the arch, between the two real contact
regions (heel and ball). Contact should be modelled as heel and ball/toe
points, with the COP as their load-weighted average.
