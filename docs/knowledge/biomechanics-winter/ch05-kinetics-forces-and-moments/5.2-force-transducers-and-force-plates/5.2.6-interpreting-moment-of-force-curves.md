# 5.2.6 Interpretation of moment-of-force curves

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §5.2.6, pp. 124–126 ·
> [open PDF at p. 124](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=137) ·
> Up: [5.2 Force transducers and force plates](./INDEX.md)

A full link-segment analysis gives a net moment curve per joint over the
movement. Winter reads the stance-phase ankle, knee and hip curves of a hip
replacement patient (three trials) muscle group by muscle group, then shows
that their extensor sum — the **support moment** — is a far more consistent
pattern than any single joint.

## Key ideas

- **Convention** (Fig. 5.14): moments are those acting on the segment
  *distal* to the joint, counterclockwise positive (subject walking to the
  right). So a plantarflexor ankle moment is **negative**, a knee extensor
  moment **positive**, a hip extensor moment **negative**.
- **Ankle:** positive (dorsiflexor) for the first ~80 ms after heel contact —
  pretibial muscles lowering the foot eccentrically; then plantarflexors take
  over, first controlling the leg's forward rotation over the flat foot in
  midstance, then peaking at about **−60 N·m** and plantarflexing the foot for
  pushoff (concentric; a major energy generation in normal gait, reduced in
  this patient). Drops to zero before toe-off because the patient unloads that
  limb onto the good one; the toe only touches for the last ~90 ms.
- **Knee:** one extensor pattern throughout stance — quadriceps control knee
  flexion in early stance, extend the knee in midstance, and act eccentrically
  to control flexion at pushoff. Peak ~+30 N·m.
- **Hip:** extensor (negative) from heel contact to midstance — helps the
  quadriceps control knee flexion and stops the trunk pitching forward over
  the hip (the hip reaction force has a backward component in the first half
  of stance). Then flexor (positive): first reversing the backward-rotating
  thigh, then pulling it forward and up to start swing. Early extensor peak
  ~−30 N·m.
- **Support moment** $M_s$: the sum of the three with extensors counted
  positive (Winter, 1980). A total-limb "push away from the ground" pattern,
  consistently positive in single support across scores of walking and
  running trials, despite large variability at the individual joints
  (Winter, 1984). Lesson: read moment curves as an integrated synergy, not
  in isolation (full treatment in Section 11.1).
- Representative paper: Pedotti (1977).

## Equations and numbers

$$M_s = -M_a + M_k - M_h \quad(\text{Fig. 5.14 legend: } M_s = M_k - M_a - M_h)$$

Fig. 5.14 (patient, stance only, heel contact 0 ms, toe-off 680 ms), read off
the plot: $M_a$ ≈ +5 early, to ≈ −60 N·m near 450–500 ms; $M_k$ ≈ +30 N·m
peaks in early–mid stance; $M_h$ ≈ −30 N·m early, ≈ +10–15 N·m late; $M_s$
rises to ≈ 60–70 N·m and stays there until ~600 ms, then falls to ~0.

## Relevance to migera

High for gait plausibility and the active ragdoll. (1) It gives the *shape*
and rough magnitudes of human stance torques (ankle up to ~60 N·m even in a
patient, ~130 N·m in Example 5.4; knee and hip tens of N·m), usable to size
per-joint PD ceilings in `src/character/anim/ragdoll.rs` in proportion
(ankle ≥ knee ≈ hip in stance) rather than by guesswork. (2) The support
moment is a one-number invariant for an inverse-dynamics test of procedural
walking: over single support $M_s$ must stay positive; a negative $M_s$ means
the animated leg is collapsing rather than holding the body up. (3) The
reason each muscle group is active is a checklist for authored gait: a knee
that flexes in early stance and re-extends by midstance, and a foot that
rolls over a braking ankle, are what these moments produce.
