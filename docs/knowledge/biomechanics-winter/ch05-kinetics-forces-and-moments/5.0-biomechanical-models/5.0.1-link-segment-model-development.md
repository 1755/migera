# 5.0.1 Link-segment model development

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §5.0.1, pp. 108–109 ·
> [open PDF at p. 108](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=121) ·
> Up: [5.0 Biomechanical models](./INDEX.md)

An inverse solution is only as good as its model. The link-segment model
replaces each body part by a rigid link with a point mass at its COM and a
constant moment of inertia, joined by pin (or ball-and-socket) joints; the
parameters come from anthropometric tables scaled by height, mass and sex.

## Key ideas

The five modelling assumptions:

1. Each segment has a fixed mass concentrated as a point at its COM (which is
   also its centre of gravity vertically).
2. The COM location within the segment stays fixed during the movement.
3. Joints are hinge (or ball-and-socket) joints.
4. Each segment's mass moment of inertia about its COM (or about its proximal
   or distal joint) is constant.
5. Each segment's length — the distance between its joints — is constant.

- Fig. 5.1 frames the whole chapter: kinematics + anthropometrics + external
  forces → **inverse solution** → joint reaction forces and net muscle
  moments → (Ch. 6) energies and powers.
- Fig. 5.2 shows the lower limb as masses $m_1, m_2, m_3$ and inertias
  $I_1, I_2, I_3$ at the COMs of thigh, leg and foot, joined by pins.
- The inverse solution is valued clinically because the effect of training,
  therapy or surgery is obvious at the moment level while often hidden in the
  raw kinematics.

## Relevance to migera

These are exactly the assumptions of migera's skeleton and of its avian
ragdoll: rigid bones of constant length, fixed COM per body, constant
inertia, ball joints. Assumption 5 is why pose data must preserve bone
length (the pose tests do). Anything the book derives from this model
therefore transfers directly to the ragdoll, and anything that violates it
(soft tissue, a translating knee axis) the ragdoll cannot express either.
