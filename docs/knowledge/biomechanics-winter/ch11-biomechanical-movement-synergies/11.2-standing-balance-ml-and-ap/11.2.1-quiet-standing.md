# 11.2.1 Quiet standing

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §11.2.1, pp. 286–288 ·
> [open PDF at p. 286](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=299) ·
> Up: [11.2 M/L and A/P balance in standing](./INDEX.md)

In quiet standing the body is an inverted pendulum. The offset of the centre
of pressure (COP) from the centre of mass (COM) sets the COM's horizontal
acceleration: COP − COM = −K·ẍ. The ankle muscles move the COP fore–aft.
Side to side, the hip abductors/adductors move it with a reciprocal
**load/unload** of the two legs. The whole thing behaves as a **stiffness**
control: the COP moves in phase with the COM, with slightly larger
amplitude. It is not a delayed reactive feedback loop.

## Key ideas

- **Outcome measures** are COP (from force plates) and COM (from a
  14-segment body model, see §4.1.7). The model needs four trunk segments
  because the lungs and heart shift mass internally.
- **COP − COM is the error signal.** It drives COM acceleration, so balance
  control amounts to controlling the COP.
- **A/P (sagittal):** the COP is controlled by the **ankle plantarflexors/
  dorsiflexors** (Horak & Nashner, 1986).
- **M/L (frontal): the load/unload mechanism.** With side-by-side feet, the
  COP is controlled by the **hip abductors/adductors** (Winter et al., 1996).
  - With two force plates, left and right vertical GRFs each oscillate about
    **50% of body weight**. Their fluctuations are virtually **equal in
    magnitude and exactly 180° out of phase**.
  - The net M/L COP is the weight-averaged position of the two foot COPs. It
    is in phase with the *right* vertical force (with the figure's sign
    convention).
  - Horizontal GRFs are negligible, so inverse dynamics puts each hip's
    frontal-plane moment in phase with that side's vertical force. The two
    hip moments are equal and opposite: raising the abductor effort on one
    side loads that leg and unloads the other, which shifts the COP toward
    the loaded foot.
- **Stiffness, not reaction** (Winter et al., 1998). The COP is virtually
  *in phase* with the COM, oscillating with a slightly larger amplitude on
  either side of it. The error COP − COM therefore always accelerates the COM
  back toward centre. The left and right abductors are kept at a small tone,
  so tiny sway produces synchronized hip-moment fluctuations large enough to
  hold balance. Because no continuous reactive loop is needed, the reactive
  sensors stay "on standby" for unexpected perturbations.
- **What drives the COM fluctuations** is partly internal: respiration and
  the heartbeat shift trunk mass (Winter et al., 1966; Hunter & Kearney, 1981).

## Equations and numbers

Inverted-pendulum relation, valid for sway angles **< 8°** (Winter et al., 1996):

$$\mathrm{COP} - \mathrm{COM} = -\frac{I\,\ddot{x}}{W d} = -K\,\ddot{x} \qquad (11.3)$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $I$ | moment of inertia of the whole body about the ankle, in the direction of interest (sagittal for A/P, frontal for M/L) |
| $\ddot{x}$ | horizontal acceleration of the COM in that direction |
| $d$ | vertical distance from the ankle joints to the COM |
| $W$ | body weight above the ankle joints |
| $K = I/(Wd)$ | constant with units of s² |

This is Eq. 5.9 / 5.10 of §5.2.9 with $h \to d$. The sign says that when the
COP lies *behind* the COM, the COM accelerates *forward*.

*Order of magnitude (derived here, not in the book):* for a point mass at
height $d$, $I = m d^2$, which gives $K = d/g$. With $d \approx 0.9$ m,
$K \approx 0.09$ s². A distributed body has a somewhat larger $I$, so $K$ is
roughly 0.1 s². A 1 m/s² COM acceleration therefore needs the COP about
10 cm from the COM.

Fig. 11.4 (one subject, 16 s of quiet standing), read off the plots:

- Left vertical force ranges ~46–56% BW and right ~44–54% BW, each
  fluctuating by a few % BW about 50%.
- The net M/L COP displacement stays within about **+0.8 / −1.2 cm**.

## Relevance to migera

- **Idle sway amplitude and shape.** `phase.rs`'s `standing_idle()` layers a
  slow (~7 s) lateral weight shift. Real *quiet* standing COP moves only
  about ±1 cm M/L, and the weight on each foot moves only a few % of body
  weight. A visible multi-second weight shift is a deliberate postural change,
  not quiet-standing sway. Keep the always-on sway at centimetre scale, and
  treat a larger shift as an occasional event. The source also names breathing
  as a real driver of sway, which supports coupling a small sway component to
  the breath clock.
- **Built (2026-09-29).** `PhaseLayer::standing_idle` now sways the pelvis
  ±6 mm side to side and ±4 mm front to back (on the breath clock) over
  planted feet, trunk carried upright (`stance::sway_over_feet`), in place
  of bending the spine over a fixed pelvis. Headless on the rendered rig the
  centre of mass travels ~1.2 cm side to side; live, the feet hold to
  0.1 mm (`phase::tests::the_standing_idle_sways_the_body_over_planted_feet`).
- **Occasional weight shifts, built (2026-09-29).** `QuietSway::weight_shift`
  cuts time into 14 s slots (the first always square). A fixed hash gives
  each slot square, left or right, eased in and out over 1.6 s.
  `stance::shift_weight` poses it: pelvis 4.5 cm toward the loaded foot, a
  4° roll down on the resting side, spine rolled back level, both legs
  re-solved to the feet. On the rendered rig that is COM 3.4 cm over, loaded
  knee 14.1° against resting 25.9°. Live, the pelvis spans 57 mm while the
  feet move 0.1 mm. For the feet to hold on screen, not only in the target,
  the hips had to spring as fast as the legs
  ([a lagging pelvis rotation slides planted feet](../../../character-animation/ik-and-locomotion/a-lagging-pelvis-rotation-slides-planted-feet.md)).
  A/P sway now tips the body about the ankles as an inverted pendulum: the
  pelvis drops as it leans, so each hip keeps its distance to its ankle.
  Level, a 4 cm lean had lifted the feet 3.3 mm.
- **Push recovery, built (2026-09-30).** `balance::Balance` is Eq. 11.3 with
  the COP law `COP = COM + s·x + b·ẋ` (the in-phase stiffness above, plus
  damping), clamped to the feet (`balance::Support`, and no further than
  the model's 8°). It is posed with the ankle lean A/P and the load/unload
  shift M/L. Live, 0.25 / 0.2 / 0.3 m/s pushes peak at 2.3 / 2.2 / 3.2 cm of
  pelvis and return, feet ≤ 1.3 mm. See
  [push recovery as Winter's pendulum](../../../character-animation/ik-and-locomotion/push-recovery-is-winters-pendulum.md).
- **M/L weight shift is a pelvis motion driven at the hips.** To shift weight
  onto one leg, the pelvis translates toward that leg with a slight frontal
  tilt: one hip "lifts" and loads its leg while the other unloads. The ankles
  play almost no part in this. In the kinematic stack this is a pelvis
  lateral offset, with the legs' IK absorbing it (`pelvis.rs`, `legik.rs`),
  not a whole-body lean about the ankles. A/P sway is the reverse: the body
  pivots about the ankles as one rigid pendulum.
- **A standing controller for the active ragdoll.** Today the ragdoll root is
  kinematically pinned, and `GravityScale = 1 − strength` hands the load to
  the pin (`ragdoll_plugin.rs`), so nothing balances. An unpinned
  standing ragdoll needs Eq. 11.3 as its model: pick a desired COM
  acceleration $\ddot{x}^* = -k_p(x - x_0) - k_d\dot{x}$ and place the COP at
  $x - K\ddot{x}^*$. A/P gets its COP through ankle torque, M/L through
  opposite hip abduction torques on the two legs. The book's finding that
  healthy balance is a stiffness (PD-like, in-phase) law and not a
  delay-heavy reactive one supports doing exactly this with the existing
  quaternion PD. The gains must obey the explicit-integration bound.
- **Feasibility check.** The COP can only be placed inside the support
  polygon. The largest COM acceleration standing balance can produce is
  roughly (half foot length)/K ≈ 1 m/s² A/P. A push that needs more must be
  answered with a step (see [11.3.1](../11.3-dynamic-balance-during-walking/11.3.1-inverted-pendulum-in-steady-walking.md)).
  That estimate is derived here, not in the book.
