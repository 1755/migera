# 6.0.5 Negative work of muscles

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §6.0.5, p. 144 ·
> [open PDF at p. 144](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=157) ·
> Up: [6.0 Introduction](./INDEX.md)

Negative work happens in an **eccentric** contraction: the muscle moment
opposes the joint's motion, so moment × angular velocity is negative. Some
external force — gravity or the ground reaction — produces a joint moment
larger than the muscle's, the muscle lengthens under tension, and energy
flows **from the limb into the muscle** (absorption).

## Key ideas

- Fig. 6.5a: flexor moment (+) during extension (−) → negative power.
  Fig. 6.5b: extensor moment (−) during flexion (+) → negative power.
- The external force does work *on* the muscle; the muscle acts as a brake.
- Typical gait examples: the knee extensors controlling knee flexion at
  weight acceptance, the knee flexors decelerating the swinging leg before
  heel contact (Fig. 6.17 phases K1 and K4 in
  [6.3.1](../6.3-internal-and-external-work-calculation/6.3.1-internal-work-calculation.md)).
- Negative work still has a metabolic cost, though lower than equal positive
  work ([6.1](../6.1-efficiency/INDEX.md), Eq. 6.11).

## Equations and numbers

$$P_m = M_j\,\omega_j < 0 \iff \operatorname{sign}(M_j) \neq \operatorname{sign}(\omega_j)$$

## Where to read in the book

- p. 144 (PDF 157): definition and Fig. 6.5 (a), (b).

## Relevance to migera

A PD joint that is damping or braking motion is absorbing in exactly this
sense. In a natural gait most absorption is concentrated at weight
acceptance (knee) and at the end of swing (knee flexors decelerating the
shank); absorption spread uniformly across all joints and all phases —
what a heavily damped spring stack produces — is not the human pattern.
