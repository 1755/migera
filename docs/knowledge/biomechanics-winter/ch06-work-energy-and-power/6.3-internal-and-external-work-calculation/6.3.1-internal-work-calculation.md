# 6.3.1 Internal work calculation

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §6.3.1, pp. 162–167 ·
> [open PDF at p. 162](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=175) ·

Internal work has been computed many ways, each blind to some real muscle
work. Ranked from worst to best: summed energy increases, whole-body COM
energy, sum of segment energies, joint power, muscle power. Only joint
power (and in principle muscle power) sees where energy is generated and
absorbed; even it misses cocontraction; nothing mechanical sees isometric
holding against gravity.

## Key ideas

**6.3.1.1 Energy increases in segments.** Fenn (1929) summed the increases
in each sprinter segment's KE and PE over a stride. It ignored exchange
within segments and transfer between them, so it overestimated badly — his
sprinters came out at an average 3 horsepower. The opposite error: Saunders
et al. (1953), Cotes and Meade (1960), Liberson (1965) took the trunk's
"vertical work" (PE increases) as all the body's work, ignoring HAT's
PE/KE exchange and all the lower-limb work.

**6.3.1.2 Centre-of-mass approach** (Cavagna and Margaria 1966). KE and PE
of the whole-body COM from force-plate data. Wrong premise: the COM is a
vector (mass-weighted) average, so opposite accelerations of reciprocally
moving segments (one leg forward, the other back; arms against legs) cancel,
while their energies — scalars — should add. Underestimates (Winter 1979),
needs force-plate contact (nothing known in running's flight phase), and
cannot see cross-joint generation/absorption.

**6.3.1.3 Sum of segment energies** (Ralston and Lukin 1969; Winter et al.
1976). Sum within each segment (captures exchange) then across segments
(captures transfer), then sum absolute changes of the total (Eq. 6.24).
Still underestimates: cross-joint cancellation is invisible. Williams and
Cavanagh (1983) added empirical corrections for running.

**6.3.1.4 Joint power and work.** Integrate $M_j\omega_j$ phase by phase
([6.0.6](../6.0-energy-and-work-of-muscles/6.0.6-muscle-mechanical-power.md),
[6.0.7](../6.0-energy-and-work-of-muscles/6.0.7-mechanical-work-muscles.md))
to find every source and sink. Also captures external work automatically: an
external load shows up as larger joint moments and hence larger power.
(The book cites "Equation (6.6)" here; the joint-power formula is Eq. 6.1.)

**6.3.1.5 Muscle power and work.** Net joint moment cannot separate
agonist from antagonist. Example: $M_j = 40$ N·m, $\omega_j = 3$ rad/s →
120 W joint power; with a 10 N·m antagonist cocontraction the agonists
actually generate 50 × 3 = 150 W and the antagonists absorb 10 × 3 = 30 W.
Joint power underestimates both positive and negative work. Fixing it needs
per-muscle force (the indeterminacy problem of
[5.3.1](../../ch05-kinetics-forces-and-moments/5.3-dynamic-bone-on-bone-forces/5.3.1-muscle-force-indeterminacy.md))
and muscle velocity; Morrison (1970) did four muscles in walking, Yack
(1986) the three major biarticular groups.

## Equations and numbers

$$W_b = \sum_{i=1}^{N}|\Delta E_b| \quad[\text{J}] \tag{6.24}$$

$E_b$ total body energy (Eq. 6.23) at each of N sample intervals.

$$W_m = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} F_m\cdot V_m\,dt \tag{6.25}$$

$F_m$ muscle force, $V_m$ muscle lengthening/shortening velocity.

**Runner's knee, Fig. 6.17** (Winter 1983, slow jog, stride ≈ 0.7 s,
peak knee extensor moment ≈ 210 N·m):

| Phase | When | What | Work |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | early stance | knee extensors absorb as knee flexes under load (peak ≈ −480 W) | −53 J |
| K2 | mid/late stance | knee extensors shorten under tension | +31 J |
| K3 | late stance / early swing | braking backward rotation of leg and foot as thigh drives forward | −11 J |
| K4 | late swing | knee flexors decelerate the swinging leg and foot before heel contact | −24 J |
| K5 | just before heel contact | small burst flexing the leg, slowing forward motion to near zero | +5 J |

Absorption (53 + 24 J) dominates; the knee extensors generate only 31 J.

## Gait power bursts (where energy is generated and absorbed)

Chapter 6 poses these as Problem 4b ([6.5](../6.5-problems-kinetic-kinematic-data.md))
against Appendix Table A.7 (Winter's single-subject walking trial,
right-leg heel contact frame 28, toe-off frame 70, next heel contact frame
97; 1 frame = 0.0143 s). Values below were read from Table A.7:

| Joint | Phase (frames) | Sign | Peak | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ankle** | 29–31, just after HC | − | −23.5 W (fr 30) | dorsiflexors lower the foot |
| Ankle | 38–55, mid-stance | − | −32.6 W (fr 50) | plantarflexors absorb as the leg rotates forward over the planted foot |
| **Ankle** | **57–69, push-off** | **+** | **+272.4 W (fr 64, ≈6 frames before TO)** | **plantarflexor push-off — by far the largest burst in the stride** |
| Knee | 32–36, weight acceptance | − | −55.8 W (fr 35) | extensors control knee flexion (K1) |
| Knee | 38–44 | + | +21.0 W (fr 40) | extensors re-extend the knee (K2) |
| Knee | 63–70 and on into early swing | − | −62.9 W (fr 67) | extensors limit knee flexion around toe-off (K3) |
| Knee | late swing (fr 13–25 of the table) | − | −62.4 W (fr 22) | flexors decelerate the swinging leg (K4) |
| Hip | 28–30, at HC | + | +44.0 W (fr 28) | extensors, "push from behind" (H1) |
| Hip | ≈36–64, mid/late stance | − | −16.7 W (fr 42) | hip flexors absorb as the thigh extends |
| Hip | 65–70 and early swing (fr 2–9) | + | +51.4 W (fr 2) | flexors pull the thigh into swing, "pull-off" (H3) |

At push-off Table A.7 also lists a joint transfer "leg to foot" of
−387.8 W at frame 64 (negative: energy crossing the ankle from foot to
leg), i.e. push-off energy flows back up into the leg that is starting to
swing. For the named, body-mass-normalized averaged curves (A2-S, K1-S,
K3-S, K4-S, H1-S, H3-S) see
[7.4.5](../../ch07-three-dimensional-kinematics-and-kinetics/7.4-kinetic-analysis-reactions-and-moments/7.4.5-sample-moment-and-power-curves.md).

## Relevance to migera

- **Pick joint power as the metric.** For judging a generated gait, the sum
  of segment energies (Eq. 6.24) and COM energy both hide exactly the
  failure modes procedural animation produces (joints fighting, limbs
  moving reciprocally). Per-joint $\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\boldsymbol\omega_\text{rel}$
  on the ragdoll, or after inverse dynamics on the kinematic walk, is the
  right level; cocontraction must be measured separately
  ([6.1.1](../6.1-efficiency/6.1.1-causes-of-inefficient-movement.md)).
- **Where push-off should come from.** In human walking the dominant energy
  source is the ankle plantarflexors in the ~10% of the stride before
  toe-off, with a secondary hip-flexor pull-off into swing and a hip-extensor
  push just after heel contact; the knee is mostly an absorber. A migera
  walk whose forward progression is supplied by root motion (as in
  `src/character/anim/locomotion.rs`) has no such source. For an active
  ragdoll that is meant to walk under its own torques, this table is the
  target shape for the ankle, knee and hip PD power curves.
- **Test oracle.** The COM-vs-segment contrast is a ready unit test for any
  energy code: two bodies swinging in antiphase have zero COM energy change
  but nonzero summed segment energy change.
