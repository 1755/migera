# 6.1.1 Causes of inefficient movement

> **Source:** Winter (2009) §6.1.1, pp. 151–154 ·
> [open PDF at p. 151](../../../../books/bmcoh/BIOMECHANICS_AND_MOTOR_CONTROL_OF_HUMAN.pdf#page=164) ·
> Up: [6.1 Efficiency](./INDEX.md)

Rather than chase efficiency directly, Winter (1978) lists four mechanisms
that waste metabolic energy; removing them improves efficiency
automatically. All four are cases of muscles doing work that does not end up
as useful net body motion.

## Key ideas

### 6.1.1.1 Cocontraction (pp. 151–152)
Antagonists fighting each other. If a movement needs a net 30 N·m flexor
moment, the efficient way is 30 N·m of flexors alone; 50 flexor + 20
extensor gives the same net but 20 N·m of pointless effort on *both* sides
— flexors doing positive work only to cancel the extensors' negative work.
Seen strongly in hemiplegia and spastic cerebral palsy, mildly in normal
movement where a joint must be stiffened (heavy lifting, the ankle in
walking and running). Net joint moment cannot reveal it; only antagonist
EMG can, and without EMG-to-tension calibration it is not truly
quantifiable.

### 6.1.1.2 Isometric contraction against gravity (pp. 152–153)
In normal dynamic movement, momentum lets energy interchange smoothly and
little effort goes into merely holding segments up. Slow pathological
movement holds limbs or trunk nearly isometrically for long periods: a
crouched CP patient's quadriceps holding a flexed knee, or a crutch-walking
child holding her leg off the ground before swing-through (Fig. 6.10). No
movement, so no mechanical work — invisible to any work calculation; only
calibrated EMG could estimate its cost, and no valid technique exists.

### 6.1.1.3 Generation at one joint, absorption at another (pp. 153–154)
The least understood: one muscle group does positive work while another,
elsewhere, does negative work at the same time — a body-wide version of
cocontraction. **Normal walking example (Fig. 6.11):** in double support
the trailing leg's plantarflexors push off (positive work) while the leading
leg's quadriceps and tibialis anterior absorb energy in weight acceptance
(negative work). Instability in pathological gait makes this much worse.
The only way to see it is joint-by-joint power and the overlap of
simultaneous positive and negative phases. It is not a defect in normal
gait: push-off and weight acceptance are both essential to walking safely.

### 6.1.1.4 Jerky movements (p. 154)
Efficient movement looks smooth (ballet for art, high jump for
performance): energy added by positive work is kept, and little is lost to
later negative work. A CP child's jerky gait is the opposite — energy added
is removed a fraction of a second later, a succession of stops and starts,
each burst costing metabolic energy. Assess it by segment-energy work
([6.3.1](../6.3-internal-and-external-work-calculation/6.3.1-internal-work-calculation.md) §6.3.1.3)
or joint-by-joint power (§6.3.1.4).

## Equations and numbers

Constant moments:

$$\%\text{COCON} = 2\times\frac{M_\text{antag}}{M_\text{agon}+M_\text{antag}}\times100\% \tag{6.12}$$

The factor 2: each unit of antagonist moment forces an equal extra unit of
agonist moment. Example: 20 N·m extensor antagonist, 50 N·m flexor agonist
→ 40/70 = 57%.

Time-varying activity (Fig. 6.9, two antagonist activation profiles over a
stride):

$$\%\text{COCON} = 2\times\frac{\text{common area }A\,\&\,B}{\text{area }A+\text{area }B}\times100\% \tag{6.13}$$

For tibialis anterior (A) vs soleus (B) over one walking stride, 24%. (The
book's text says "Equation (5.12)" and "Figure 5.9" here; both mean 6.12 and
6.9.)

## Relevance to migera

This is the most directly usable part of the chapter for **flagging
unnatural procedural motion**, each cause mapping to a measurable signal:

- **Cocontraction ↔ PD stiffness.** A ragdoll holding pose by high kp is
  "cocontracting" — lots of torque, little net motion. Eq. 6.13 applied to
  the positive and negative parts of per-joint torque over a stride gives a
  stiffness index; human ankle ≈ 24%.
- **Isometric holding ↔ static torque.** Torque with ~zero joint velocity
  does no work, so a work metric will not see it; track time-integrated
  |torque| separately if effort matters.
- **Cross-joint cancellation ↔ simultaneous opposite-sign joint powers.**
  Expected only in double support (trailing ankle generating, leading knee
  absorbing); elsewhere in the cycle it indicates joints fighting.
- **Jerk ↔ alternating power bursts.** Sum of |ΔE| per segment
  ([6.2.1](../6.2-forms-energy-storage/6.2.1-segment-energy-and-within-segment-exchange.md))
  or of |P| per joint per stride rises sharply for stop-start motion; spring
  overshoot, foot-lock pops and phase-transition snaps in `gait.rs` /
  `transition.rs` would show as extra high-frequency power reversals.
